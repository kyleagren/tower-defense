# Tower Defense Project — Design Doc

A living scratchpad. Sections get filled in / revised as ideas solidify. Contradictions and open questions are flagged rather than resolved prematurely.

## 1. High-Concept

A single-player-first (co-op required eventually) tower defense game combining:

* Dungeon Defenders: player has direct combat presence in the fight, can't solo-carry the defense, fixed-kit characters with distinct identities.
* Path of Exile: towers use a skill-gem-style system — a base skill (e.g. Fireball) is a projectile definition, and separate support/modifier gems transform or attach behavior to it (multi-projectile, volley, returning, nova, pierce, etc). Composition over inheritance.

Map structure: fixed path (classic TD track, not open-grid pathfinding), with multiple lanes (Dungeon Defenders-inspired) rather than a single lane. This matters mechanically: the player can only be at one lane at a time, so towers can't rely on player presence as a fallback for bad behavior — they need to hold their own lane reasonably well on their own (see Section 3, Targeting).

### 1a. Match Structure

* **Lore update (decided): the "crystal" is the mage himself, sheltering in a force field.** Rather than a separate DD-style Eternia Crystal object, the defended objective is the mage's own shell/barrier — enemies that reach it attack him directly, and when his health hits zero, the level ends. This was deliberately chosen to solve two problems at once: it moves the loss condition away from a 1:1 Dungeon Defenders copy (an earlier point of distaste), and it gives an in-fiction reason the mage isn't part of the active fighting roster (Section 2) — he's tied to sustaining the shell, not idle/twiddling his thumbs while the player fights. Mechanically this doesn't change anything already decided below (same continuous-attack, same "loses when it falls" behavior) — it's a flavor resolution of `DefendableObjective` (Section 11), not a new mechanic.
  * Open: multi-crystal maps (see below) still need an answer for how one mage covers more than one objective point at once. One idea floated, not decided: the mage spectrally projects his presence to multiple shell locations at once. Deliberately left open for now.
* Loss condition (decided, fully resolved): a shared crystal (Dungeon Defenders' Eternia Crystal-inspired, though the specific object/flavor is now leaning toward the mage's shell — see lore update above) that all lanes converge on. Confirmed: enemies physically attack/camp the crystal over time (DD-style continuous attack), not an abstracted single-hit leak — this creates a natural "last stand" zone around the crystal where all lanes converge, which the player's limited mobility benefits from as a fallback.
  * Multiple crystals on harder maps (decided as a campaign-progression lever, see below). Spreads the same fixed capacity across more simultaneous points of failure — a map/objective-design lever rather than an enemy or tower power lever.
  * Resolved door-problem questions: losing any one crystal ends the run (not "all must fall"). Enemies are not pre-assigned to a specific crystal — they threaten whichever is closest to them. This creates a nice emergent interaction with battlefield-shaping CC (Section 6): an enemy rerouted/delayed near a lane boundary could end up threatening a different crystal than the one it started toward.
* Wave count (decided): varies by map, scaling up on later/harder maps — consistent with difficulty progressing primarily via map selection.
* Tower capacity (corrected — fixed, not growing): capacity is a fixed ceiling for the entire match, not something that grows over waves (this reverses an earlier draft of this section). Pacing/escalation comes entirely from the economy: a player could technically afford full capacity turn one, they just won't have earned enough currency yet. Simpler than a scheduled-growth model — one fewer system to design/tune.
* Enemy complexity escalates over waves — simpler ground threats early, more complex/varied threats later (e.g. flying enemies, akin to DD's kobolds vs. wyverns). Pays off earlier decisions like the anti-air base-skill concept and targeting runes (Section 3) — relevant later in a match, not mandatory from wave one.
* Clarified: there are two distinct difficulty vectors, and they need different tools.
  1. Campaign progression (map-to-map). Difficulty rises as the player advances through maps — via richer enemy compositions, multiple crystals, more complex layouts. This vector is resolved: it's compositional/structural, consistent with "difficulty via composition, not stats," and doesn't require any power-scaling mechanism since the map itself changes.
  2. Difficulty-setting scaling (replaying the same map at Easy/Medium/Hard/etc.). This is the vector still causing trouble. Enemy HP/composition can be inflated per setting easily enough, but if the player's own toolkit is completely identical regardless of difficulty, skill and gem-tier breadth alone may not be a satisfying enough answer — TBD, still actively needed.
* New direction for difficulty-setting scaling: player-character progression, separate from towers. The player explicitly wants some form of persistent player-character power growth (stats, gear, something) — valuing the classic RPG fantasy of returning to a map that used to be hard and now comfortably farming it. Key insight: every rejected idea so far (star-upgrades, tower leveling, tower stat-stick itemization) was specifically about towers — nothing has been decided about the player character's own combat stats (their personal health/damage/movement, separate from the tower/gem system). This is a genuinely unexplored axis, not a reopening of a settled question, and could resolve the difficulty-setting vector without touching the tower system's build-expression-only philosophy at all.
  * Guardrail (tightened, confirmed): player character growth must never outstrip tower power — towers remain the dominant, primary source of the defense's strength regardless of character progression.
  * Revised direction — "mage XP" unlocking global tower buffs (decided, replaces pure player-character-stat framing above). Rather than (or alongside) the player character's own combat stats growing, the architect/mage gains persistent XP that unlocks a buff slot applying a passive, global modifier to all towers. This is honestly a reintroduction of tower power scaling, but through a structurally different vehicle than what was rejected: a global, meta-progression layer (architect-level, persistent across matches) rather than a per-tower one (avoiding the messy "which tower gets my gem, what if it's destroyed" complexity that per-tower scaling created).
  * Anti-grinding safeguard (decided): tie meaningful mage-XP gains to difficulty cleared, not matches played/repetition — mirrors the existing gem-tier system's difficulty-gated (not repetition-gated) access. Directly answers the player's own self-identified worry ("grinding lower content into a demon") by making the only path to real progression be facing harder content, not farming easy content repeatedly.
  * Buff variety, not a single flat number (decided) — this is the key fix for "boring, just equip biggest number." Multiple distinct buff types to choose from (tower range, cooldown recovery, attack speed, etc.) rather than one universal "+power" stat. Different types matter differently depending on what's actually built that match (attack speed favors trigger-chain/combo-heavy defenses, range favors sniper-style spread layouts, cooldown recovery favors CC-wall-heavy setups) — turns the choice into a real build-identity decision rather than a scalar, consistent with "trade-offs not gates" (Section 6) applied to meta-progression.
    * Guardrail: no single buff type should be objectively best regardless of build — needs genuine differential value across playstyles to avoid becoming a new "one optimal pick."
    * Concrete mechanism proposed: talisman drops with a randomized stat budget, ARPG-loot style (not a fixed selectable menu). Each difficulty level drops talismans with an equal total stat budget for that tier, spread across stats — e.g. Level 3: +30 to all stats, plus a randomized large bonus (~90) to one specific stat; Level 4: +40 to all, ~100 bonus to one stat. Which stat gets the big roll varies per drop.
      * Why this avoids "biggest number" within a tier: since every drop from the same tier has an equal total budget, no single talisman from that tier is objectively better than another — only better for a given build's preferred stat.
      * The genuine decision this creates is across tiers, not within one: a well-rolled lower-tier talisman (e.g. a huge attack-speed roll from Level 3) can legitimately compete against an unrolled higher-tier one (bigger total budget, but hasn't rolled the desired stat yet) — a classic, well-established "hold a good roll vs. gamble on an upgrade" loot-chase tension, distinct in texture from a fixed-menu choice but not a worse form of decision-making.
      * Equip model: appears to be one talisman equipped at a time (compare a new drop against what's currently held, decide whether to swap) — consistent with the earlier lean toward one or two slots max.
    * Open sub-question: how many buff slots can be active at once — one (forces a real single identity choice) vs. multiple (risks reconstructing "stack everything" flat power creep)? Leaning toward one or two max, not decided.
* Multi-lane awareness UX (resolved): a minimap showing monster positions and which lane/spawn point enemies are coming from.
* Co-op economy (resolved): capacity and currency both shared during the match (simplifies balance to one number instead of two interacting pools); any leftover currency split evenly at the end — even split rather than contribution-based, specifically to avoid incentivizing competing for kills over covering weak points in a cooperative game.

## 2. Player & Characters

* Player can deal direct damage to enemies, but cannot hold the entire defense alone — towers are the backbone.
* Player has their own health.
* Player death (resolved): temporary respawn with a cooldown. An ally can revive faster than waiting out the timer (co-op). If not revived in time, the player can spend match-currency ("mana," working name) to skip/reduce the wait. This is a good, confirmed use of excess match-currency — but explicitly not treated as fully resolving the currency-sink TBD below, since it's a situational spend (only relevant on death) and more uses are still wanted.
* Undecided: whether player can buff/repair/reposition towers (leaning yes).
* Design choice: characters are fixed-kit, not gem-built — a small roster (~2-5) of hand-designed characters with specific abilities, distinct roles/identities.
  * Rationale: keeps the "too much freedom → one optimal build" problem contained to towers, while characters give the game identity and pre-match strategic choice ("this map needs the tank").
  * Player-facing decision becomes: pick character (fixed identity, made before the match) + build tower loadout (flexible, made during the match).
* Set bonuses for characters — mentioned as a progression idea, not yet fleshed out (see Itemization).
* Mage-style character concept — corrected framing: the mage is not intended to be one of the normal playable combat characters with his own fighting kit. He's structurally distinct — "the architect" — a role who places/preps towers (and, per the tokens mechanic below, pre-crafts tokens) before a wave, then leaves; he is not part of the fighting roster. This gives him a complete, self-contained identity: his gameplay is entirely about forward-thinking prep (predicting where the defense will be weak, without knowing exactly what's coming) rather than a fighting kit. **Lore resolution (see Section 1a): he "leaves" because he's sustaining the shell/force field that is itself the level's loss condition — not idle between waves, just not fighting.**
  * Term idea: "runes" as the in-fiction name for support gems, at least for this character's flavor.
* Build phase timing: towers can be built both in a pre-wave build phase and during active waves — not restricted to build-phase-only like classic Dungeon Defenders/TD structure. Clarified: this is a universal player capability, available regardless of which character is chosen — not gated to any one character (e.g. not mage-only). Explicitly not going the Dungeon Defenders route of each character placing/owning their own separate towers.
* Resolved mechanic for mid-wave building — "inscribed tokens/stones": the player cannot build freely during a wave. Instead, between waves, the mage pre-crafts a small number of specific, already-defined towers (not generic build charges) as tokens — up to a standing cap (e.g. max 3) — based on anticipating likely weak points in the defense for the upcoming wave. These can be recrafted/replaced between waves as the player learns more. During the wave itself, the active fighting character can reactively place these pre-crafted tokens where needed.
  * This makes prep genuinely predictive rather than purely optimizing a known layout: the mage has to guess where and what kind of help will be needed without full information, and the payoff/failure of that guess plays out during the wave.
  * Resolves the earlier replenish question: tokens are a standing cap with per-wave recrafting — always up to 3 banked, refreshed/adjusted between waves based on what was learned, rather than either a free reset or a fully scarce run-long resource.
* Fighting character roster sketch (initial pass, not final):
  * Tank — can taunt/aggro monsters to pull them off the defenses; directly embodies the "shore up a weak spot" fantasy from the original high-concept.
  * Assassin — stealths around, aims to unleash burst damage on tankier monsters specifically.
  * Sniper — ranged, sustained damage from range; explicitly flagged as possibly overlapping too much with the assassin conceptually. Not yet resolved — if differentiated, positioning/playstyle (mobile burst-and-retreat vs. stationary sustained DPS) is the likely axis, not damage type. Deferred for later.

## 3. Tower / Modifier ("Gem") System

* Towers fire skills built from a base skill + stacked modifiers pipeline, PoE-style.
* Architecture direction: base skill produces a data definition (damage, speed, count, on-hit behavior, etc); each modifier transforms that definition and/or hooks into events (on-hit, on-expire, on-tick). Composition, not subclassing.
* Tag system (decided): adopting a Path of Exile-style tag system — skills carry tags (e.g. Projectile, AoE, Duration, Trigger, and elemental tags), and runes/supports can require or add tags. This generalizes the modifier pipeline so a rune can apply to "anything with tag X" rather than needing bespoke logic per skill×rune combination, and fits the multiplayer serialization goal well (tags are simple enums/flags, easy to sync).
  * Related: a general "on-trigger"/"on-break" event hook is planned (see Section 3a) so that one skill's trigger event can fire a separate skill — reuses the same event-hook pattern as other on-hit/on-expire modifiers, and enables emergent combos (e.g. wall + delayed burst) without needing bespoke per-combo features.
  * Elemental types are being added as tags, for build-crafting depth, not for enemy-resistance reasons. This resolves an earlier tension cleanly: elemental typing on skills (late-game element-conversion runes, character gear that buffs a given element) and elemental resistance on enemies are separable decisions — the bookkeeping/forced-reconfiguration cost that made resistance a bad idea (see Section 6) comes entirely from the enemy side. The plan is to build a rich elemental tag system for player-facing build depth while keeping enemies resistance-free.
  * This is an architecture decision, not a balance decision — exact numbers (how strong is an element-conversion rune, etc.) don't need to be known now; the tag-based data model supports tuning later without a structural rewrite.
* This data model needs to be clean/serializable from day one — see Multiplayer section, this is the highest-stakes architecture decision in the project.
* Gems are permanently available at low tier; higher-tier/rarer versions of the same gem can be found/dropped, and the player has a limited number to allocate across towers — creates a real allocation decision ("which tower gets my one Tier 3 Nova gem") without needing tower/skill XP.
* Leaning against tower/skill leveling (numbers-go-up-over-time regardless of choice) — feels like it undermines the build-expression point of the whole system. Not fully settled.
* Resolved: towers don't scale in power at all within a match — capacity is fixed (see Section 1a), and difficulty access is gated by player skill and toolkit breadth (gem-tier unlocks), not by tower power growth.
* Elemental reactions (Genshin Impact "swirl"-inspired): a rune/effect that checks whether a target already carries one elemental tag and triggers a bonus effect if a second element is applied — this is a direct, low-cost application of the tag system already committed to above, not a separate new system.
* Base skill roster sketch (initial pass, not final): fireball, lightning bolt, meteor, a wind-based skill (swirl-style elemental reaction potential, see above). Ice leans toward crowd-control rather than pure damage. CC-supporting rune example: an "electrocuted" effect that briefly (~0.5s) prevents movement, addable to non-CC base skills like lightning bolt via a rune.
* Player-buff towers (new category, resolves the long-open "how does the player fit into combat" question) — e.g. a "Buff Totem"/aura-style base skill with no damage output, buffing the player character instead of attacking enemies. Answers Section 2/9's open question by making support-vs-carry a build/capacity-allocation choice rather than a fixed role: a player who wants to be more of a "main character" dedicates part of their capacity to these towers instead of pure damage towers.
  * Does not violate the "player must never outstrip tower power" guardrail (Section 3a): the buff still costs capacity — the same finite resource that would otherwise fund a damage tower. This is a reallocation of the fixed budget, not a net-new power source layered on top of it, which is the key distinction from the rejected star-upgrade/uncapped-mage-XP ideas.
  * Self-limiting without needing a new rule: a player who over-invests in buff towers necessarily under-invests in actual defense, and since the player can only be in one lane at a time and explicitly can't solo-carry, an all-buff build gets punished by whichever lane isn't currently benefiting from their presence — the existing capacity/multi-lane/no-solo-carry systems already guard against this becoming degenerate.
  * Moddable by runes like any other tower (a rune could widen radius, strengthen the buff, add a second buff type) — reuses the existing pipeline rather than requiring a new system.
  * Open design fork: should the buff be range-based (player must stay near the tower to benefit — creates a positioning incentive, reinforces the "player is a scarce resource" theme already central to multi-lane design) or global (simpler, no positioning interaction)? Leaning range-based for thematic consistency, not decided.
  * Complementary to, not redundant with, the mage-XP talisman system (Section 3a) — that buffs towers, this buffs the player. Different subjects.
* Targeting priority (decided approach): compared to Bloons' explicit "strong/first/last" targeting dropdown (which Dungeon Defenders doesn't have), the plan is:
  * Base skills have a natural/default targeting inclination tied to their identity (e.g. a fireball naturally seeking flying enemies, or a dedicated anti-air spell existing as its own base skill) — turns targeting into a composition decision (which base skills to bring) rather than a per-tower micromanagement menu, fitting the action-oriented feel over a Bloons-style spreadsheet-TD approach.
  * A dedicated targeting rune/gem slot lets players override/refine that default when a specific wave calls for it — consistent with the existing modifier pipeline (targeting logic is just another property a modifier can transform, architecturally no different from a damage or projectile-count modifier) and preserves the "spend a slot on this, at the cost of another modifier" trade-off already central to the gem system.
  * Rejected relying purely on player intervention as a substitute for tower targeting: with multiple lanes (see Section 1), the player can only be at one lane at a time, so bad targeting elsewhere on the map can't be corrected by player presence — towers need to be locally competent on their own most of the time, not just when the player happens to be standing there.

### 3a. Battlefield Shaping / Crowd Control

* Revisited and resolved: towers themselves CAN be destroyed after all — but the itemization-loss problem is fully solved by the mechanic's design. Originally rejected due to two problems: what happens to attached runes on destruction, and how mid-combat repair works. Now resolved:
  * Common case: disabled, not destroyed. Enemy attacks on a tower temporarily disable it (implies a repair mechanic, giving repair the purpose originally wanted) rather than removing it outright.
  * Even in the worst case (true destruction), attached runes return to the player's inventory rather than being lost. This fully resolves the original itemization-loss concern — a precious, limited-tier gem is never actually lost, only the time/positioning investment of re-placing the tower. This isn't a compromise on the original CC-only decision, it's a clean fix that gets back DD-style stakes (towers going down matters) without the harsh downside.
  * Ties to enemy design: a specific enemy archetype ("sapper," see Section 6a) is the one that meaningfully threatens towers, rather than any monster incidentally being able to — consistent with "trade-offs not gates": the player can see a sapper coming and choose to prioritize it, rather than tower loss being random collateral.
* Ice wall spell — iterating on the exact mechanic:
  * Considered a trigger/landmine version (invisible until a monster steps on it, then the wall appears), costing a rune slot to equip. Initially unsure this was meaningfully different from an always-present wall, since it consumes the same build-limit/rune-slot cost either way and behaves the same once active.
  * Resolved: the trigger version has real, distinct value as a combo enabler. Found the actual use case: the surprise of an invisible trigger is specifically what causes enemies to bunch up (an always-present wall gets anticipated/routed around by an approaching group; a hidden one doesn't). Chaining a second, separately-triggered delayed spell (e.g. a bonus-damage burst) off that clustering creates a genuine combo payoff that an always-there wall can't replicate — the wall's job shifts from "block" to "create a clustering moment for something else to exploit."
  * Architectural implication: this points toward building a general "on-trigger"/"on-break" event hook (similar to Path of Exile's "Cast on X" trigger gems) rather than a bespoke wall-specific feature — one skill's trigger event can fire a second, separate skill. This reuses the same hook already needed for the explosion rune, and turns "wall + delayed burst" into one emergent combo among potentially many, rather than a special case built once.
  * Decided: support both formal linking, and distinguish between event types precisely. Confirmed there should be a way to explicitly pair two skills via a rune ("cast X when this breaks"). Also clarified that "on-activate" (fires when a trap/trigger is first stepped on) and "on-break" (fires when the object is destroyed) are two distinct event hooks, not interchangeable — both are planned. Adding new distinct event types like this is expected to be straightforward given the compositional/tag-based architecture already committed to.
  * Open implementation note for later: as more distinct event types accumulate (on-hit, on-expire, on-tick, on-activate, on-break, etc.), the tag system should extend to gate which skills expose which events (not every skill has a meaningful "on-break," e.g. a skill with no HP) — the same tagging principle already used to gate which runes apply to which skills, applied to events too. Not an immediate problem, just worth keeping in mind to avoid nonsensical or dead rune combos later.
  * Explicit stated goal behind this whole line of discussion: thinking broadly about desired systems upfront — not needing to enumerate every possible gem/rune now — specifically to avoid the project painting itself into a corner later. The compositional architecture (tags + general event hooks) is what makes this front-loaded breadth actually cheap rather than speculative over-engineering.
  * Direction A — regrowing wall: the wall periodically regrows a few seconds after being broken, providing sustained lane pressure/chokepoint attrition rather than a one-time block.
  * Direction B — proximity trap: triggering causes the wall/damage effect to spawn directly under the triggering monster rather than at a fixed spot — a personal, single-target punish (area denial + burst damage tied to enemy positioning) rather than a lane-wide uniform obstacle. Considered meaningfully different from Direction A, not just a reskin, since it targets an individual enemy rather than blocking a lane.
  * Both directions are being kept open as they solve different problems (sustained pressure vs. single-target burst punish) — not yet decided whether both, one, or neither ships.
  * Cooldown/recharge for any wall-like spell: decided on a hybrid — recharge on break/trigger, or after a max duration, whichever comes first. Pure break-only recharge risks a dead-resource lockout if a wall is placed somewhere no enemy ever reaches (distinct from, and worse than, the wall simply being a wasted/ineffective placement, which is expected and fine).
  * A rune that makes the wall explode on trigger/break is also planned — fits the existing modifier-pipeline pattern (hooking into an on-break/on-trigger event).

## 4. Tower Capacity / Economy

* Decision: build-capacity limit (Dungeon Defenders style), not income limit (Bloons style).
  * Rationale: income-as-limiter creates snowball economies (early efficiency compounds). Capacity-as-limiter keeps tension on composition (which towers/gems, in which slots) rather than economy optimization — fits the build-crafting focus better, and is a cleaner problem for co-op (shared/individual capacity vs. shared/individual income).
* Income Tower idea: a tower that shoots a "spirit" that gets absorbed by an enemy; the player must personally kill that specific enemy within a time limit to cash in the bonus income.
  * If the player doesn't/can't get there in time, the bonus is lost (self-limiting, no hard cap needed).
  * Enemy carrying the spirit is immune to tower damage — must be player-killed — either via a sword item the tower grants, or innately for characters who don't need it (e.g. a sniper-type character).
  * This makes income an active decision with opportunity cost (attention spent here = attention not spent elsewhere) rather than a passive scaling number — self-balancing against the "trivialize the game by farming gold turn 1" problem because the limiting factor is player attention/skill, not an arbitrary cap.
  * Open question: how many income towers/spirits can be "in flight" at once, and does that interact with tower capacity too?
* Two-currency split (decided): a separate match-only build resource (spent to place towers/tokens, doesn't carry over) from long-term progression currency (e.g. gold earned from completing missions).
  * Rationale: a single shared currency for both building and progression would incentivize under-using build capacity in order to hoard currency for later, which is a bad incentive to create — punishing players for using their full capacity well in the moment. Splitting the two removes that conflict entirely: full capacity can be used freely without it costing anything long-term.
  * This is separate from the earlier "prevent instant-full-build" pacing question — that can likely still be handled by the build resource simply not being available all at once (e.g. accrues over time or per-wave), whether or not it's shared with progression currency.
  * Decided: passive accrual, not active collection. Considered a Dungeon-Defenders-style active collection mechanic (colored "soul" particles from kills, sized/colored by value, walked over to collect) but rejected it: per-kill resource-value feedback isn't very actionable anyway, since towers auto-target and the player usually can't act on "which kill gave more." It would also create real visual clutter given this game's core fantasy is AoE/multi-kill modifier payoffs (nova, pierce, etc.) — many simultaneous kills would mean many overlapping value-coded particles at exactly the moment the player should be enjoying a clean payoff, not parsing clutter.
  * Death feedback still wanted, decoupled from resource value: keep a simple, uniform death effect (particle/sound) for combat "juice" regardless of resource granted — satisfying without encoding information nobody can act on mid-fight.
  * Optional future addition: granular resource-per-kill info (e.g. broken down by enemy type) could still be surfaced in a non-real-time way, like an end-of-wave summary or stats panel, for players who want to dig into it without cluttering live combat.
  * Long-term progression currency/system shape is still fully undecided (see Section 5).
  * TBD — excess match-currency sink. Once capacity is full, match-currency has no remaining purpose for the rest of a match. Instant-repair and instant-token-refresh were both rejected (didn't land). One good confirmed use has been found: spending currency to skip the player-death respawn timer (Section 2) — but this alone doesn't fully resolve the gap, since it's situational (only relevant on death) and won't absorb excess currency in matches where death is rare. Still actively looking for additional uses.
  * **Decided: a "spell table"/crafting-table currency sink.** Instead of PoE-style named currency items (exalts, annuls, etc.), the player spends gold at a shopkeeper/crafting-table to put an item in and randomly remove a mod or add a mod — non-deterministic, gold-gated crafting rather than deterministic orb-based crafting. Explicitly kept in scope, distinct from the deterministic-crafting veto in Section 5: that veto ruled out a full PoE-style multi-currency system where named orbs each do one precise, predictable thing; this is a single gold-gated random reroll action, a much smaller system. Rationale: items should be interesting because the player can act on them, not just be found — this is the mechanism for that. Also doubles as an answer to the excess-currency-sink TBD directly above, since it's gold-funded and available regardless of match state (not situational like the death-respawn skip).

## 5. Itemization / Long-Term Progression

Explicit goal: avoid flat "+X% tower damage" stat-stick itemization (feels like it doesn't change decisions, just numbers).

Directions being considered:

* Capacity-affecting items — e.g. reduce a specific tower's capacity cost, or grant an extra gem slot on a chosen tower. Interacts directly with the capacity system rather than sitting beside it.
* Gem-slot items — an extra modifier socket on a specific tower, functions as itemization but reads as build-expansion.
* Character-specific unlocks — items/progression that grow a character's fixed kit slightly (new ability) rather than boosting stats — fits the fixed-identity character design.
* Set bonuses tied to playstyle — e.g. rewards a specific strategic approach (support-focused loadout) rather than being strictly better in all situations.
* Tiered gem drops (see Section 3) as the core progression loop — limited allocation of upgraded gems across towers.
* Meta-progression sketch (loose, borrowing from Dungeon Defenders): multiple difficulties, a possible time limit imposed on later difficulties, difficulty-gated unlockable items, multiple maps. Connected and decided: higher difficulties gate access to higher-tier gems, which (combined with player skill) is the actual answer to "what stops a player from playing the hardest difficulty on day one" given towers don't scale in power (see Section 1a).
* Run/session structure (decided): DD-style — pick a mission/map, play it, return to a hub. Not roguelike as the primary structure. A roguelike mode is being kept in reserve as a possible future alternate game mode, not built now — consistent with the project's general pattern of not overcommitting early while keeping ambitious options open (same pattern as the multiplayer and art-direction decisions).
* Survival/endless mode (loose, same "future alternate mode" treatment as roguelike) — not committed now. Likely cheap to build later relative to other alternate-mode ideas, since it's expected to mostly reuse the existing wave-generation system (enemy composition/count/timing parameters) run indefinitely with escalation, rather than needing new bespoke mechanics.
* Performance-based meta-progression rewards (confirmed direction, specifics open): architect-XP/talisman rewards should scale with how well a difficulty was cleared (e.g. crystal health remaining, waves survived cleanly, time taken), not just whether it was cleared. Exact bonus conditions and magnitudes not yet decided.
* Open to ARPG-style loot generally (talismans, uniques) but wants to think more concretely about what the loot pool looks like before committing — not yet decided.
* Scope discipline: PoE-style *deterministic* crafting (named currency orbs, each with a precise, predictable effect) explicitly considered and vetoed. Recognized as too much scope for a solo project — consistent with a broader pattern of rejecting overly complex systems throughout this design (mana-orb collection, matchmaking co-op, building roguelike now instead of later, star-upgrades, capacity-scaling). **This does not rule out item modification generally — see the decided spell-table crafting mechanic under Section 4 (a single gold-gated random add/remove-a-mod action), which is the adopted, smaller-scope resolution to "items should be interesting because the player can act on them."**
* Behavior-changing unique items for towers (e.g. a unique granting a meteor tower a second meteor, beyond what a rune could do) — considered, with a real identified risk and a proposed fix:
  * Risk: a powerful tower-specific unique could push players toward always building around that one tower, undermining the build-diversity goal the whole modular rune system exists to protect.
  * Key principle for whether this risk materializes: depends on how many roughly-comparable uniques exist across different base skills. A wide, balanced roster = good variety (different uniques excite different runs/sessions). A small handful of clearly-dominant uniques = bad convergence (everyone builds the same thing). The design goal is breadth and balance across uniques, not avoiding them.
  * Proposed fix (stronger, more robust than relying on unique balance alone): a cap on how many of a given tower type can be fielded at once, with items that can raise that cap. This doesn't limit how powerful a tower+unique combo can be — it limits how much of total capacity that combo can consume, structurally forcing continued diversification even with an extremely strong unique. A cap-raising item becomes a genuine "commit harder to this specialization" choice rather than something needed by default. This is a natural extension of the already-existing "capacity-affecting items" direction above, not a new category.

## 6. Enemy Design Philosophy

Core principle: enemies should create trade-offs, not gates.

* Avoid: hard counters / immunities (e.g. "immune to single-target," "requires pierce to hit at all"). These force specific builds the same way over-tuned gems would force one optimal build — same failure mode, moved from gem system to encounter design.
* Prefer: soft pressure — stats/behavior that make certain approaches more efficient without making others unviable.
  * Tanky/slow vs. fast/fragile — rewards different damage shapes without gating.
  * Grouping vs. spreading behavior — rewards AoE/pierce more, doesn't disable single-target.
  * Bursts of fast enemies — rewards attack speed/multi-target without disabling slow high-damage builds (just riskier).
  * Partial resistances (e.g. -50% from a damage type), not binary immunities.
* Conclusion: enemy roster doesn't need to be very complex relative to the modifier system — the modifiers already provide combinatorial depth. Enemies mainly need to provide pressure, not puzzles. A relatively small, well-tuned roster should be enough.
* Rejected: elemental resistances on enemies (e.g. fire/lightning resistance to discourage single-element spam). Even partial resistance imposes a real bookkeeping/forced-reconfiguration cost on the player (checking matchups before every wave) without enough strategic payoff to justify it, and it would require committing to a full elemental-typing system across every tower just to support it. Existing levers (above) already provide enough enemy variety without this cost. (Elemental typing is still being added — see Section 3 — but purely as a skill/build-crafting axis, decoupled from enemy resistance.)
* Difficulty tiers should differentiate via composition, not just stats. Extends the above principle to difficulty settings themselves: reserve certain enemy types/combinations or tighter wave timing for higher difficulties, rather than only scaling HP/damage multipliers. Purely-stat-based scaling risks the "bullet-sponge" problem — higher difficulty feeling like the same fights, just slower, rather than meaningfully distinct.

## 7. Multiplayer (Co-op)

* Hard requirement, not optional — must ship with multiplayer eventually.
* Scope: invite-a-friend co-op only, not public matchmaking. This significantly reduces cost (no lobbies/matchmaking infra, no anti-cheat, no region servers) — likely a Godot 4 high-level multiplayer setup (one player hosts, direct connect or something like Steam networking).
* Approach: build single-player first, but architect everything as if multiplayer exists from day one.
  * Simulation/state logic must be separated from rendering/input from the start (not retrofitted later).
  * The modifier/gem data model must be serializable/replicable-friendly (plain data — numbers, enums, references — not a tangle of node references and ad hoc signals).
  * Authority questions (e.g. who "owns" a placed tower) should be consciously decided per-system as placeholders, even if unenforced until multiplayer is actually built (e.g. default to host-authoritative).
* Remaining real cost even for friend-coop: still need a client/authority model, still need a sync strategy for the modifier system specifically (complexity risk scales with system complexity, not player count), still roughly doubles testing/debugging surface vs single-player.
* Economy split (decided): tower capacity and match-currency are both shared between co-op players during a match (simplifies balance to one shared pool rather than two interacting per-player pools). Any leftover currency is split evenly at the end — not contribution-based, to avoid incentivizing competing for kills over covering each other's weak points.
* Role structure during prep/combat (resolved): during prep, both players are simultaneously independent architects ("two mages"), each building/prepping within the shared capacity and currency pool. Each player runs to a physical, diegetic console to lock in their fighting character once ready — duplicate character picks are allowed (both players could choose the same character). Consistent with the project's pattern of giving mechanical processes physical, in-world presence (mage casting/inscribing, the crystal, income spirits).
  * Natural consequence worth being aware of: since both mages spend from the same shared pool simultaneously, there's an inherent "who gets to spend first" dynamic — expected to be handled by normal player coordination in a cooperative game, not treated as a problem requiring a system-level fix.
* Pause (resolved): allowed in single-player (useful given the amount of moment-to-moment decision-making during waves); not really feasible in co-op, consistent with most co-op games.

### 7a. Engine Choice

* Comparing Godot, Unreal Engine 5, and Unity.
* Background: real C++ experience, a few tutorials in both UE5 (incl. some Blueprints) and Godot, ~0 practical hands-on hours in either; would prefer not to learn C# (for Unity) but confident it's learnable; has hardware (desktop + a laptop with i7-10750H, 16GB RAM, mobile RTX 2060) capable of running any of the three comfortably; cares strongly about the game running/optimizing well.
* Key considerations weighed:
  * UE5: best fit for existing C++ knowledge and for the composable modifier system logic; most mature multiplayer/replication tooling; strongest 3D pipeline; but heaviest engine and steepest overall learning curve, and default tooling/settings lean toward high-fidelity production values that need to be actively opted out of for a lightweight stylized project.
  * Unity: C# required (net-new language); strong 2D/3D middle ground with a lighter default performance posture well-suited to a stylized, non-photoreal project; DOTS/ECS is a genuinely good architectural fit for the simulation-heavy load this game will have (many enemies/towers/projectiles/modifiers updating every frame); mature multiplayer options (Netcode for GameObjects, Mirror, Fish-Net) with a lot of solo-dev precedent; large indie community/tutorial base.
  * Godot: closest language fit (GDScript ≈ Python), lightest/fastest iteration, fully free/open-source with zero licensing considerations ever — but least mature 3D and multiplayer tooling of the three, which are two of this project's hardest technical problems.
  * Licensing/revenue at realistic hobby-project scale is a non-issue for any of the three: Unity Personal free tier covers up to $200k/year revenue; UE5 is free until $1M lifetime revenue (5% royalty above that); Godot is free/MIT forever. Not a deciding factor here.
* Current lean: Unity.
* Decision reversed — final choice: Godot. After starting implementation in Unity (a spike-test Rune/Tag/MultiProjectileRune), decided against Unity specifically due to distrust of the company following the 2023 pricing controversy — a trust-based decision, independent of the technical comparison above. Explicitly acknowledged and accepted: this means taking on the two hardest problems in the whole design (3D, and networking a novel modifier system) using the engine previously flagged as least-proven for exactly those things. Considered a conscious tradeoff, not an oversight.
* Language within Godot: C# (via the .NET-enabled Godot build), not GDScript, for performance reasons — given the simulation-heavy load this game will have (many lanes, enemies, projectiles, per-frame targeting/modifier resolution), and given prior comfort with C# (the original Unity spike was written in C#) plus a C/C++/Python background. Decided to start directly in C# rather than write GDScript first and port later, since the .NET setup is unavoidable overhead either way and starting in one consistent language (not mixing GDScript+C# within the spike) avoids the real interop-boundary costs that come from crossing between the two languages carelessly. Mixing languages later (C# for specific hot-path subsystems, GDScript elsewhere) remains a possible future optimization, to be decided with real profiling data once the architecture is proven — not a decision made now.

## 8. Art

* No 3D modeling experience; doesn't want to hire an artist or use generic asset packs for this solo project.

## 11. Preliminary Class/Object Architecture (early sketch, not final)

Not a commitment — a first pass at what objects the codebase will likely need, to think through composition/inheritance shape before writing code. Expect this to change once the first spike test is built.

* Spell — the unified "recipe" concept: base skill data (damage, speed, tags) plus applied Rune modifiers and event hooks. Independent of how it manifests in the world.
  * Correction (surfaced during implementation): Spell must be split into an immutable definition and a separate, per-instance runtime wrapper. Since Spell is a Resource, many towers referencing the same Spell asset would share the exact same object in memory — if `attachedRunes` lived directly on Spell, attaching a rune to one tower's spell would incorrectly affect every other tower sharing that same asset. Fix: Spell stays a pure, immutable Resource (tags, base stats, `CreateBaseContext()`), with no rune list on it at all. A new plain class, SpellInstance (not a Resource), wraps a reference to a shared Spell definition plus its own private `attachedRunes` list, and owns `TryAttachRune`/`DetachRune`/`Resolve` (moved off of Spell itself). Each tower gets its own SpellInstance wrapping a shared Spell definition — rune loadouts stay independent per tower while stats/tags stay safely shared. This also means `Spell.CreateBaseContext()` had to become public instead of protected, since SpellInstance is a separate class calling it rather than a subclass — a small, deliberate loosening of encapsulation traded for solving the shared-mutation bug.
  * Base tags vs. effective tags (implementation note, surfaced while coding Spell). A Spell's own tags (e.g. Fireball = Projectile + Fire) should stay hardcoded/immutable per subclass — these are inherent to what the base skill is. Tag-modifying effects (e.g. a unique that converts Fireball's element to Lightning) should NOT mutate this base tag list — instead, they're just another rune-like effect attached to the spell instance (reusing the Rune transformation mechanism, per the Item/Modifier note below). A future "effective tags" property, computed from base tags + whatever's currently attached, is the right extension point when conversion effects are actually built — not needed for the spike test, and doesn't require reworking the hardcoded base tags now. Open nuance for that future point: does conversion replace a base tag or add one alongside it — not yet decided, not urgent.
  * Rule for where a new stat's authored base value lives (established while implementing Range and Cooldown): if every spell needs the concept regardless of delivery type (Range, Cooldown), it's declared once on the abstract Spell base as an ordinary `[Export]` property — Godot's serialization is per-asset, not per-class, so every concrete subclass still gets its own independently-set value despite the shared declaration. If a stat only makes sense for one delivery type (Damage, ProjectileCount, Radius — all projectile-specific), it stays on the specific subclass instead. The corresponding resolved-context fields follow the same split: Range and Cooldown live on the abstract SpellCastContext base (so runes can modify them regardless of spell type — e.g. a rune trading bonus damage for added cooldown), while Damage/ProjectileCount/Radius stay on ProjectileCastContext.
* CastEffect family — a small set of implementations handling delivery: `ProjectileEffect`, `ZoneEffect`, `WallEffect`/`TrapEffect`, possibly `BeamEffect`/`AuraEffect` later. A Spell's tags determine which CastEffect resolves it (a Projectile-tagged skill uses `ProjectileEffect`; a Wall/Trigger-tagged skill uses `WallEffect`) — a direct extension of the existing tag-gating system, not a separate mechanism. Not fully settled as the final approach, but good enough to start with and revisit after the first spike test.
* Rune — its own class: tier, required/granted tags, the data transformation it applies, any event hooks it registers. Referenced constantly elsewhere but worth being explicit that it's a first-class object, not just an attribute of Spell.
* TriggerBinding — pairs a trigger condition (on-activate, on-break, on-hit) with a Spell to cast in response — makes trigger-chaining (Section 3a) inspectable data rather than hardcoded per-effect logic.
* StatusEffect — temporary state living on an Enemy (an elemental tag for the swirl-reaction system, the "electrocuted" immobilize, the income-spirit-carrier flag). An Enemy holds a list of these rather than each being special-cased into the Enemy class.
* Tower — HAS-A SpellInstance (composition, not inheritance) — wrapping a shared Spell definition, per the correction above. Tower owns position, capacity cost, HP/disabled state; SpellInstance owns the currently-attached runes. Keeping them separate makes "runes return to inventory on destruction" clean (detach the SpellInstance, discard the empty Tower shell) and lets the Spell definition be shared safely across many towers — a mage-crafted token is a SpellInstance not yet attached to a placed Tower; a player-buff tower is just a Tower + SpellInstance using an aura-flavored CastEffect, not a new top-level type.
* PlayableCharacter — recommend a thin shared base (position, input) rather than a combat-shaped base the Architect awkwardly inherits from. Fighting characters get a `CombatComponent`; the Architect gets a `BuildComponent` instead — composition over a deep inheritance chain, which also matches Godot's node/composition idioms directly.
* Enemy — recommend mostly one generic, data-driven class (stats loaded from a data asset) rather than a subclass per enemy type, since most of the roster (fodder/tank/ranged/flyer) differs only by stats and a few behavior flags. Reserve actual subclassing/behavior modules for the few enemies with genuinely unique logic — the Sapper (paths toward towers, applies disable) and the income-spirit carrier (immune to tower damage) are the real candidates.
* DefendableObjective — generic name for the crystal-or-whatever loss-condition object, chosen so the visual/flavor can change without renaming code. Flavor is now leaning resolved (see Section 1a): the mage's own shell/force field, not a separate crystal prop — the generic name/code shape doesn't need to change either way, which is exactly why it was named generically.
* Item (decided, unifies Talisman and Unique) — a single equippable-to-character class covering both meta-progression buff items ("talismans," name WIP) and tower-behavior-changing uniques, rather than two parallel item systems. One inventory/equip system to build instead of two.
  * Modifier — an Item holds a list of these. Recommend one Modifier class with a `SlotType` enum (Prefix/Suffix/Unique) rather than three separate subclasses — prefix and suffix are behaviorally identical at runtime (a +40 stat prefix and +40 stat suffix do the same thing once rolled); the distinction is a generation-time constraint (how many of each an item can roll), not a runtime behavior difference, so subclassing would be overkill.
  * Unique modifiers reuse the Rune transformation mechanism, not a separate system. A unique modifier like "Meteor towers drop 2 meteors" is functionally the same kind of thing a Rune does — it transforms a Spell's data definition. The difference is only where it's triggered from: a Rune is manually slotted into one specific Tower instance; a unique Item's modifier applies passively/automatically to any matching tower type built, sourced from an equipped character Item. Recommend the actual effect-application logic be shared with Rune's transformation interface rather than duplicated.
  * Crafting tools/systems for Items are explicitly deferred for implementation — not needed to establish the baseline Item/Modifier shape. The design direction is now decided, though (spell-table random add/remove-a-mod, Section 4) — building it is still deferred until Items themselves exist, but the shape it should take when built is no longer open.
* WaveDefinition, MapDefinition, Lane, EconomyManager — confirmed as sensible, no changes from the initial sketch above.

## 12. Multiplayer Architecture Implications (early sketch, not final)

Concrete implications of the class architecture above for co-op (see Section 7 for the co-op requirements this supports). The general shape of the architecture (composition over inheritance, data-driven objects like Spell/Rune/StatusEffect/WaveDefinition) is naturally friendly to this — this is expected to be an additive layer, not a rework, provided the following patterns are built in from the start rather than retrofitted.

* EconomyManager must be host-authoritative from day one. Given the decided two-simultaneous-mages co-op model sharing one currency/capacity pool (Section 7), if both clients independently mutated a local copy of that shared pool, simultaneous spends could desync the two clients' view of what's left. Pattern: one source of truth on the host; a client's spend action is a request, the host validates (enough currency? capacity available?) and broadcasts the confirmed result to both clients. Neither client mutates the shared pool directly.
* Every state-changing player action needs the same request/validate/broadcast pattern, not just currency: placing a Tower, attaching a Rune, casting a Spell, triggering a token, spending on the death-respawn skip. Building this pattern in from the first implementation of each system is much cheaper than retrofitting RPC wrapping onto many independent action-handlers later.
* Enemy simulation should be authoritative and single-sourced, not run independently per client. If both clients ran their own local Enemy AI/pathing/damage simulation, small timing/floating-point differences would let the two clients' enemies drift out of sync — a nasty class of bug to debug after the fact. The host runs the real Enemy simulation; clients receive state updates and interpolate/display, rather than computing their own version of the truth.
* Spell/Rune data syncs as small IDs referencing a static data table, not as fully serialized objects. A Tower's rune loadout should be a short list of rune IDs over the network, not embedded Rune objects with their transformation logic riding along — both clients already have that logic locally in code.
* Player death/revive needs its own explicit synced state (Section 2) — a downed player's respawn timer and "currently being revived by an ally" status need to be visible to both clients, not assumed to fall out of existing systems.
* Implementation note (Godot-specific): Godot 4's high-level multiplayer API provides mechanisms for these patterns — RPCs (`@rpc` annotations) for the request/broadcast pattern, and multiplayer authority settings for host-authoritative state, similar in spirit to what Unity's Netcode offers but with less community precedent for complex state sync (flagged as a known tradeoff back in Section 7a). This isn't building the plumbing from scratch, it's applying the engine's built-in tools to the classes already sketched in Section 11.
