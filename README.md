# Runewarder — Design Reference

_(Working title — open to change.)_

What the game currently is, and what's still undecided. Reasoning appears only where a decision is still live or where the obvious approach is wrong for a non-obvious reason. Not a history — that's what git is for.

---

## 1. High Concept

3D, low-poly stylized tower defense combining:

- **Dungeon Defenders** — the player has direct combat presence, can't solo-carry the defense, and picks from a roster of fixed-kit characters.
- **Path of Exile** — towers cast a base skill ("Spell") plus stacked support "Runes." Composition over inheritance, deep build expression.

**Theme.** The Architect is a time-traveler. Years of hopping between eras left cracks in reality behind him, and those fractures are letting monsters — and some imprisoned boss-tier figures whose bonds have weakened — loose in the periods he damaged. The campaign is him sealing each fracture. The Sentinel roster is drawn from people he met along the way, which is what lets it keep growing without needing a shared origin. The vertical slice stays in a single era; multi-era content (distinct rosters and biomes per period, not reskins) is the long-term vision.

**Engine:** Godot 4, C#. C# over GDScript for the simulation-heavy load (many enemies, projectiles and modifiers updating every frame).

**Co-op is a hard requirement** of the finished game — invite-a-friend only, no public matchmaking. Single-player is built first, but the architecture assumes co-op from day one (see Section 7).

## 2. Core Loop & Economy

Fixed-path maps, multiple lanes converging on one or more defendable objectives. Lane count is per-map.

**Four phases**, all implemented:

- **Planning** — the level opens with the player controlling their Sentinel, free to walk around with no time pressure. Ends on a ready check (below). Exists so a future Prep-phase timer has a deliberate player action to start counting from instead of beginning the instant the level loads.
- **Build (Prep)** — the player becomes the Architect and places towers within a fixed capacity budget, allocates runes, and pre-crafts up to 3 tokens.
- **Combat (Wave)** — the player controls a Sentinel with their own health. Tokens deploy reactively; towers otherwise can't be built or repositioned. Enemies advance toward the nearest objective and attack it until it falls or the wave ends.
- **PostGame** — after clearing every wave, control returns to the Sentinel so the player can walk over and collect what dropped. Returning to the hub is another ready check. A loss skips PostGame entirely and returns to the hub after a short delay.

**Every "move on" is a ready check** — leaving Planning, starting a wave from Build, and leaving PostGame. `start_wave` (G / d-pad right) readies you up and pressing it again un-readies you. The check passes once every player in the match is ready, or when a 90-second countdown runs out; the countdown starts when the host readies and is cancelled if the host un-readies, so a player who crashed or left can't hold everyone else in place forever. In single-player you're the whole match, so one press moves on immediately.

`MatchState` (`LevelContext.Match`, null in the hub) is the one owner of the phase, the ready check and whether the match is over. The match ends exactly once however many things try to end it, and anything spawned late reads the current phase rather than waiting on an event it missed. `WaveController` runs waves from `_PhysicsProcess`, so pausing the tree pauses them.

**Objectives.** The primary one is the mage's own shell — enemies attack it directly and it falling ends the run. Harder maps add **Energy Harvesters**, which function identically minus having a mage inside; this is how one mage covers more than one objective.

Tower capacity is a fixed ceiling for the whole match. Pacing comes from the economy, not capacity growth.

**Difficulty** has two vectors — campaign progression (map to map) and difficulty setting (replaying at Easy/Medium/Hard). Both resolve through enemy composition and timing, never flat stat multipliers, to avoid higher difficulty just feeling like bullet sponges.

**Two currencies.** Match-only **mana** buys towers and tokens, accrues passively, and can be spent to skip the death-respawn wait. Persistent **gold** is earned per mission and spent at the hub's crafting table and vendor (Section 6).

**Income tower.** Fires a "spirit" that an enemy absorbs; the player must personally kill that carrier in time to collect bonus income, and the carrier is immune to tower damage. One spirit in flight at a time. It also doubles as the loot-goblin for the loot-timing direction below.

**Loot timing — built, though still a tentative design call.** Most loot never drops in real time. `ItemDropManager` rolls each kill's drop chances immediately but holds the results, then sprays the whole pile out of the primary objective's position (`DefendableObjective.IsPrimary`, the mage's shell) at PostGame, ordered common-to-legendary and placed on a golden-angle spiral so items separate without needing physics. The objective is freed as the spray starts — the shield defended all level becomes the reward. A loss never fires the victory signal, so queued loot simply never spawns, extending "nothing to loot after a loss" to everything. This sidesteps the mid-combat "risk it or lose it" tension, the ground-clutter problem, and a future Build-timer squeeze on evaluating finds — at the cost of the moment-to-moment ARPG texture during combat, which is why it is worth revisiting if it does not feel good in play. The 40-slot inventory cap becomes a real triage decision at the payout moment rather than a slow background nuisance. **Not built:** the two intended live-drop exceptions — elite enemies and whichever enemy carries the income spirit — which would keep full lose-it-on-death stakes. Today every drop is queued.

**Hardcore (considered, not built).** A per-difficulty toggle rather than a difficulty of its own. A Sentinel that dies in hardcore stays dead for the rest of the wave, which makes ally revive the mode's lifeline in co-op. Hardcore also adds the Build-phase timer; the regular game has none. Loot timing follows the timer: hardcore keeps loot queued for the PostGame spray so nothing needs evaluating under time pressure, while the regular game would go back to live drops at the enemy's feet, with anything picked up kept even if the match is lost. Hardcore probably pays slightly better rewards, mechanism open. The name is open too — ARPG players read "hardcore" as permanent character death.

Minimap shows monster positions, which lane they came from, and disabled-tower status.

Co-op shares capacity and currency during a match; at the end every player gets the full gold earned, since a co-op match doesn't spawn more enemies than a solo one. It makes them tougher instead: each player past the first adds 50% to every enemy's health (`GameRules.EnemyHealthPerExtraPlayer`), so four players face 250% — otherwise extra Sentinels kill everything too fast to leave much to do. An enemy's multiplier is fixed when it spawns, so someone leaving mid-match only affects later spawns. Pause is single-player only: every player can open their own pause menu, but it only freezes the game when nobody else is in the session (Section 3, Controls).

**Loot is instanced per player in co-op.** Each player sees only the drops rolled for them, each rolled against their own Sentinel pick; an item becomes visible to everyone only once its owner drops it on the ground from their inventory, which is also how items are traded (Section 7, Multiplayer).

## 3. Characters

The player deals direct damage and has their own health, but can't solo-carry the defense. Death is a temporary respawn on a cooldown — an ally can revive faster, or the player can spend mana to skip the wait. Only the objective falling loses the match.

- **The wait and the skip both grow with deaths this match**: 5s for the first death, +5s for each after; skipping (Interact) costs 10 mana, +5 per death after the first. Repeated deaths cost more than a single slip. The count resets each level.
- **While dead** the body is hidden and off every collision layer — enemies walk through it and stop targeting it — and the player can look around but can't move, attack, use abilities, interact or pick tokens. Anything in progress (token pick mode, a placement, an armed Health Link) cancels. A countdown and the skip prompt show on screen, the skip line red when unaffordable.
- **A wave ending while dead revives outright**, into Build or PostGame alike.
- **Respawns on the spot, as a stand-in** — dying inside a pack puts you straight back into it, so a proper respawn location is still open.
- **Ally revive is not built.** `Sentinel.Respawn()` is the hook it will call.

**Movement.** No stamina and no cooldowns on either of these for now — both are free to use as often as the player likes.

- **Sprint** (`sprint`, Shift / L3) is pressed once and stays on, for 1.3× speed and a small FOV widening — latched rather than held so a controller player isn't pinning down a stick click while steering with it. It's on `PlayerCharacter`, so the Architect sprints too. It ends when the player stops moving or moves mostly backward, and for a Sentinel, on firing, aiming, swinging, blocking or rolling; after that it takes a fresh press. Sprint never blocks combat, it just can't overlap it.
- **Dodge roll** (`dodge`, Ctrl / Circle) is a Sentinel-only, grounded roll of 5m over 0.35s along the movement input, or straight ahead with none. The first 0.25s is immune to damage — checked before Tank's block, so a raised shield isn't spent on a hit the roll already avoided. Combat input is off for the roll's duration, and the capsule tumbles one full turn over it.
- **Camera pitch** runs almost straight up to almost straight down (±89°) — being tried out; the previous range stopped at 10° above the horizon.

Characters are **fixed-kit, not gem-built**, keeping the "too much freedom collapses into one optimal build" problem contained to towers. The fighting roster collectively goes by **Sentinel** (used in code too, e.g. `CharacterId`).

**Roster:** Soldier and Tank are implemented. Assassin and Templar come later — Assassin's kit isn't designed; Templar has one floated idea (something like PoE's Divine Ire, a channeled ramping-damage release).

**Kit design principles.** Not every character needs the same four-slot template (CC + mobility + buff + panic button) — forcing that shape risks every character being the same abilities reflavored. A kit skipping CC isn't a gap that must be filled per-character; the roster compensates at a wider level. Four abilities isn't a hard requirement either; the goal was "more to do than Dungeon Defenders' two," not a fixed count. A good kit mixes genuinely new mechanics this game's systems make possible (reading and manipulating an enemy's Status) with recognizable genre tropes reskinned to fit. Kits are weighed against each other by how many decisions they ask for at once, not by slot count — two abilities sharing one slot still weigh as two.

**Reserved for undesigned characters**, so later kits don't collide with ideas already spoken for: **Detonate** (an elementalist popping all elemental Statuses in a large area — distinct from Tank's fire-only Immolate) and **self-duplication/clone** (fits an illusionist better than a disciplined soldier).

**Personal per-ability reactions** are a second axis alongside the shared Reaction system (Section 9). An ability can check an enemy's current Status and do something bespoke if it matches, without needing a slot in the shared combination map — and without following that system's rules at all. The shared system is universal and symmetric; a personal reaction is scoped to one ability.

### Soldier

Deliberately keeps one foot in traditional-shooter appeal — not every character will have a gun, so Soldier is the one giving that audience a reason to pick him. He's a magic user with a magnet-adjacent school of magic, and is also implied to be from a future era, which is the in-fiction justification for the gun and the tech-flavored parts of his kit. Converge / Repel is the magic; the Visor, Health Link and Harvest are tech.

**Slots (all built):** 1 Converge / Repel, 2 Weak-Point Visor, 3 Health Link, 4 Harvest / Overdrive. A slash is two abilities sharing one key — tap for the first, hold for the second (Section 7). Counted the way kits are weighed (by decisions, not slots), that's six.

- **Passives aren't abilities.** The four slots are four _active_ abilities; ADS, headshot crits and Tank's block are ordinary character features coded on the Sentinel rather than `Ability` instances. A dual-mode Fire/Cold rifle passive was cut as more complexity than it was worth, so the rifle applies no Status and Harvest charges purely off Rituals and other Sentinels.
- **Headshot crits (built).** A hitscan shot whose line passes through an enemy's head hitbox (`Enemy.HeadCollision`, optional per enemy scene — Grunt/Marksman/Sapper/Brute have one, TrainingDummy doesn't) is a guaranteed critical hit. This is its own ray-vs-box test against the head hitbox's volume rather than trusting a raycast's closest-hit result: the head box sits mostly recessed inside the body capsule's silhouette, so it's rarely the physically closest shape even when a shot genuinely passes through it. Because crits land far more often this way, `Gun.BaseCritDamageBonus` is lower than the shared default — 125% total on a crit versus Tank's 150%.
- **Weak-Point Visor (built).** While active, exposes weak points on every enemy at once — chosen over a single-target lock for simplicity. Each enemy lights `WeakPointCount` of its pre-placed body-part markers for the duration: Head always, when that enemy has one wired, so there's a guaranteed high-value target; the rest drawn at random from body/arms/legs. A second press ends it early. Hit detection is the same geometric-proximity test as headshots — how close the shot's line passes to the marker, not a physics hit — and a hit stacks the Visor's weak-point multiplier (1.3×) on top of whatever the crit roll already produced, so weak points and headshot crits do compound. Every shot type goes through it — rifle, Overdrive and Health Link spikes.
  - **Visor state is scene-wide on `LevelContext`**, which is what lets an enemy spawning mid-duration expose its own markers on `_Ready`. It's counted rather than a flag, so two Soldiers can overlap visors without the first to end stripping the other's markers. Only the first activation exposes markers — a second would re-roll every enemy's markers out from under the first player — so if two visors with different marker counts overlap, the first one's count holds until both end.
- **Health Link (built).** Activating arms the ability rather than firing it: the next two shots each fire a **spike bolt** — the spike itself in flight, from the muzzle toward the crosshair at 60 m/s, swept as a ray each frame so it can't tunnel through a thin enemy. A spike sticks in whatever it hits: an enemy's shoulder, or tip-first in terrain or an Ice Wall. A bolt that reaches its 30m range without hitting anything is lost. Two spikes in two different living enemies link them — a thick green beam — and they mirror each other's incoming damage for 7s. Anything else is a **dud**: a thin red beam between whatever spikes landed for 1.5s, then everything clears. Not a shared HP pool — a pair of beefy enemies standing together effectively doubles incoming damage on both. Mirrored damage is **true damage** (`DamagePacket.IsTrueDamage`): it's a copy of damage already mitigated once on the way in, so it must never be mitigated again on the way out. It also can't crit, since no roll happens on the receiving end, and doesn't carry a Status across — the partner wasn't the one actually hit by the element.
  - **Arming captures the attack binding**, which is why `Ability.CapturesAttackInput` and `Sentinel.CanUseWeaponInput` exist. **Both shots are always fired** — a first bolt that misses doesn't end the cast, the second is still owed. Backing out before the first bolt is fired is free; once it's fired, hit or miss, the cast is committed and pays the full cooldown however it ends — a dud, the 10s arming window timing out, or backing out. Clicks while a bolt is in flight are swallowed, so bolts never overlap. Punishing a player for spiking something that was about to die is the point, so a free re-arm can't be allowed to dodge it.
  - **Each spike deals 1.3× the rifle's per-shot damage** through the ordinary shot roll — it can crit, headshot and hit weak points, checked along the bolt's last stretch of flight — so spending the second shot on a dud still hits for something. It's physical, so it sets no Status. The second spike's damage lands _after_ the link forms, so it mirrors onto the first enemy.
  - **A spike in an enemy that dies just disappears**, and the beam hides once either end is gone. Losing the first target while arming costs that spike but not the cast; losing either one during a real link ends it. Terrain spikes clear when the cast ends, with a 10s self-destruct each as a backstop.
  - **The beam colors its own material**, since the tether scene's material is shared by every instance and recoloring it would recolor every other player's beam.
  - **The mirror is a hook on a debuff (`HealthLinkDebuff`), not ability state** — `OnHostDamaged`, fired from `Enemy.TakeDamage` after the health subtraction but before the death check, so a killing blow still mirrors. The mirrored hit is a `HitType.Mirrored` hit, which never reaches debuffs — that's what stops it bouncing back through the partner's own half. It keeps the original hit's `Attribution`, so the credit stays with whoever dealt it. A side effect worth knowing: that same guard means **links never chain** — damage reaching an enemy through a link stops there and isn't relayed onward to anything that enemy is separately linked to.
  - **The spike stands upright in a shoulder, not along the shot that planted it.** A spike aligned to the bullet points back at whoever fired it, which foreshortens to nothing from the one angle the shooter is guaranteed to be looking from. It parents to the enemy's `ShoulderAttachment` bone so it rides the walk cycle — **a new enemy type with that export left unwired gets its spike in the middle of its chest**, since the fallback is centre mass. Even upright, picking a marked enemy out of a live wave is hard; see the note on marker readability in Section 9.
  - **Each linked pair is two independent halves**, one on each enemy pointing at the other, rather than one shared object. The pair is symmetric by construction and neither half needs to survive the other's death. Duplicate links on the same pair are refused unless at least as strong as the existing one: equal refreshes the duration, weaker does nothing at all, so two players can't take turns re-casting a weak link to hold a strong one open forever.
- **Harvest / Overdrive.** Tap harvests; hold on a full gauge starts Overdrive, and holding on anything less does nothing. Two separate abilities (`HarvestAbility`, `OverdriveAbility`): Harvest owns the gauge, and Overdrive spends it through a reference to Harvest. A giant damage beam was rejected for the spend — too close to Templar's Divine Ire — and so was a temporary elemental blade, which would pull the roster's gunner into melee and onto Tank's turf.
  - **Harvest** pulls the **Status** off every enemy within 8m. Each one is worth 4% of the gauge (at most 20% per cast) and 5% of the Soldier's max Shield (at most 25% per cast), so Harvest's cooldown sets the fastest possible Overdrive and how much Status the Rituals keep up decides how close to it you get. Shield is a share of the Soldier's own maximum, so with no Shield gear a harvest still charges the gauge but grants nothing. A cast that finds nothing still pays its cooldown. Taking a Status the Rituals wanted is an accepted, occasional cost — they re-apply it constantly.
  - **The gauge** tracks how much of each element fed it. A cast capped by the per-cast limit or the gauge's ceiling scales every element's share down together. It's kept across waves and through death, and empties only when Overdrive spends it.
  - **Overshield.** Shield granted past the maximum becomes overshield (Section 6).
  - **Overdrive** loads **10 shots**: one click per shot, a fixed 0.5s between them that ignores attack speed, and any left unfired expire after 20s. It takes over the attack binding while loaded, and Harvest can't be tapped until it's done. Escape doesn't end it — it opens the pause menu as usual — but death does. Each shot **pierces 3 enemies at full damage**, and every enemy it passes through gets its own crit roll, headshot check and weak-point check.
  - **Damage** is weapon DPS (the weapon's final damage × final attack speed, all its mods included) × seconds of DPS per shot (1s). Basing it on DPS rather than per-hit damage keeps a fast gun and a slow heavy one on equal footing. Weapon DPS is safe to read straight off the weapon only because attack damage and attack speed currently roll on weapons alone — revisit if either ever rolls elsewhere. Ability-damage stats are meant to multiply in once they exist.
  - **Element.** The gauge's element proportions lock in when Overdrive starts and split each shot's damage. Each shot applies the default ailment of the most-fed element: Fire → Burn (half the shot's Fire damage over 3s), Cold → Chill (30% for 3s). Lightning → **Shock** (takes increased damage) and Water → **Drenched** (reduced attack speed; placeholder name) aren't built, so shots led by those apply nothing yet.
  - **Shots set no Status** (`HitType.Unmarked`) — otherwise they'd paint fresh Status for Harvest to take straight back. They still reach ailments, so a Health Link mirrors them.
  - **Health Link layers on top.** Overdrive stands aside while any other ability is capturing the attack binding, so arming Health Link mid-Overdrive takes the next two clicks as spikes and Overdrive resumes after.
  - **HUD.** The gauge is a pill at the bottom right with an `N/100` label, filled in the color of the most-fed element (a prismatic fill is a someday idea). During Overdrive it drains a tenth per shot; the shots themselves show at the crosshair (Section 4, field HUD).
  - **Talent/mod hooks (not built):** a Harvest bias toward one element; converting all charge to Wind so every shot Swirls, since Wind never becomes a Status Harvest could take naturally; and stripping ailments as well, each worth far more charge than a Status — a hybrid ailment credits each of its elements evenly. What gets fed into the gauge buys frequency, never a bigger shot.
- **Converge / Repel.** Tap Converges, hold Repels. Either one drops a 5m field for 5s on the ground under the crosshair, up to 25m out — no thrown arc, so it reads as a spell rather than a deployed gadget. Aiming at a wall or the sky still lands it on the floor beneath; with no floor there at all, nothing is cast and nothing goes on cooldown. Affects enemies only, never Sentinels. Where it lands is a deliberate skill check: either one can drag an enemy toward another lane and let it rejoin there under the normal rejoin rules.
  - **One field at a time, one cooldown.** The two are separate abilities (`ConvergeAbility`, `RepelAbility`, both on a shared `PlacedFieldAbility` base), but they're cooldown partners, and neither can be cast while the other's field stands. The cooldown (8s) starts when the field ends.
  - **Converge** pulls every enemy inside toward the center like a spring and roots it there. The root is a short `ConvergedDebuff` (speed × 0) the field refreshes every tick, so an enemy that leaves — or outlives the field — walks free a moment later without anything releasing it. **A rooted enemy still attacks:** a Marksman keeps shooting, and a Grunt hits whoever walks into reach.
  - **Repel** pulses every 0.5s, throwing whatever is inside at that moment, so enemies walking in go in small batches; the first pulse lands 0.5s after it's placed. Directions are quantized to an **octagon aligned to the caster's facing**: each enemy goes along the outward normal of the side whose wedge it's in, and the four sides on those axes (60° wedges) are wider than the diagonals (30°), so most enemies fly forward, back, left or right and the scatter stays readable. Scattering enemies away from AoE Rituals is the intended cost of choosing it over Converge.
  - **Strength** is fixed for now; it's meant to scale with ability-damage stats, so two Soldiers with different gear cast noticeably different fields. Heavier enemies are moved less by both (Section 5), never made immune.
  - **Placeholder visuals:** a translucent disc showing the radius and a small glowing core — blue for Converge, orange for Repel.
  - **Names:** "Grasp" is a maybe for Converge, and "Scatter" for Repel.
- **Considered, not slotted:** a rune-configured super-shot whose effect depends on the socketed rune, reading each Rune's own numbers directly rather than routing a gunshot through the spell pipeline. Would need a narrow new `Rune` hook, not a pipeline change.

### Tank

A melee bruiser built as the deliberate opposite of Soldier: higher max health, no ranged option at all. Move speed matches everyone else — a baseline penalty was tried and reverted because the Architect-to-Combat handoff already reads as a sudden slowdown.

- **Sword swing** (`attack`) sweeps the sword in an actual orbit around the body's vertical axis, not a local rotation, alternating direction each swing so chained attacks read as one continuous pendulum. The blade points radially outward to maximize reach and flattens parallel to the ground for the swing. A grace window after each swing — independent of attack speed, so faster attacks don't shrink it — lets a follow-up continue the pendulum instead of snapping back to idle.
- **Shield block** (`alt_action`, held) orbits the shield from its resting position at the character's side to directly in front of the body, so it's genuinely between the attack and the body rather than just reoriented in place. The next hit taken while raised is fully negated and starts a brief total-immunity window (1.5s), after which block goes on cooldown (5s). Blocking and swinging are mutually exclusive; move speed drops while raised but not during the immunity window. Shield color is the state cue — bright during immunity, dull red during cooldown.
- **No impact VFX on a landed swing, deliberately.** Soldier's `ImpactFlash`/tracer are a gun's VFX and Tank never needed a melee equivalent.
- **Known gap:** every number (`AttackDamage`, `AttackSpeed`, block timings) is a first guess, never balanced against real waves.

**Kit (built).** Fire-themed, built around a heat dial that trades damage for mitigation. Slots: 1 Shield Slam, 2 Retaliation, 3 Vent, 4 Immolate. Every number is a placeholder.

- **Heat (built)** is a signed dial from −100 to +100 with equilibrium at 0: the heat side above it, the armor side below. Heat adds extra Fire to the sword — 0.2% of attack damage per point, up to 20% at +100 — while armor falls; the armor side raises armor instead, so the Tank is either hitting hardest or at his toughest, never both. Armor multiplies by 1 − 0.2 × heat/100, so ±20% at the two ends. It never drifts: built, heaviest first, by Retaliation, Shield Slam and hits taken (1 heat per 1% of max Health, before mitigation — blocked and dodged hits add none); only Vent and Immolate bring it down. It carries across waves and clears only on death. Any Fire portion makes Fire a swing's dominant element, so a hot Tank sets Fire Status on the few enemies each swing hits — accepted, and something towers can be planned around.
  - **HUD:** a hand-drawn pill in the bottom-right spot Soldier's gauge uses, filling outward from a center line — up for heat, down in light brown for armor (blue already means Shield) — with a signed label. The fill is flat rectangles clipped by a mask to the pill's shape, rather than a smaller rounded pill of its own, and the heat side is cut from a fixed red-to-orange gradient: a little heat shows red at the center line, a lot shades up into orange. It stays up through Build, reading the player's level state rather than a live Tank.
  - **The blade glows with it.** Above 0 it blends from its metal gray toward the same red-to-orange and glows brighter as heat climbs, so the Fire in each swing has a visible reason. The blade gets its own override material, since the sword's metal is shared by the grip and every Tank.
- **Retaliation (built).** For 5s every enemy that comes within 10m — newcomers included, checked every 0.1s — attacks the Tank as its top priority, overriding leash, walls, Rituals and the objective. Sappers included, since a taunt that's useless against the hardest enemy to manage isn't worth a slot. Each taunt runs to the end of the window, so an enemy caught once stays on him even if it's shoved back out; it's taunted only once per window, so a second Tank's Retaliation takes it over rather than the two trading it back and forth. He takes 40% less damage for the window, applied before armor so the two compound without ever reaching full immunity: tankier than any other Sentinel can get, not invincible. The raw damage of every hit that lands is recorded — before any mitigation, and not counting blocked or dodged hits — and released at the end as a **Fire** blast dealing 70% of it to everything within 10m, capped per enemy at 1× the Tank's max health: enough to kill small enemies but only dent big ones, so standing in a crowd of fodder never becomes the best way to kill a brute, and a reason to stack health and armor. Being Fire, it sets Fire Status on everything it hits — Immolate's setup. Kills launch outward like Shield Slam's. +40 heat lands when the blast fires, not when the window opens, so taunting never costs armor while everything is hitting him; 15s cooldown.
  - **It's a stance.** The Tank plants his feet (no moving, jumping, sprinting or rolling), holds the shield up in front, and can't swing, block or start any other ability — a Vent already running keeps going. Pressing 2 again ends it early; dying ends it too; either releases the blast on the spot (a death-blast grants no heat, since death clears it). Ending early doesn't release the taunts — enemies stay angry for the rest of the original window.
  - **Visual:** a circle on the ground for the whole window that charges from faint red to solid orange (placeholder for molten fissures; a sword-stabbed-into-the-ground pose is planned but skipped for now), then an orange pulse at the blast.
  - **Ability mods:** Area of Effect grows the taunt and blast radius, Duration the window, Damage the blast (before the cap).
- **Shield Slam (built).** A 120°, 4m frontal cone: every enemy inside takes 1× the sword's attack damage — rolled like a swing, so it can crit and carries heat's Fire — and is knocked straight away from the Tank with the ordinary enemy knockback (Section 5), 2400 kg·m/s. Mass does the scaling: an 80kg Grunt slides about 5m, a 300kg brute barely over one and stays packed nearby where the rest of the kit wants him. Kills get a ragdoll launch (half a Fireball's). +20 heat, landing after the hits so the slam's own Fire comes from the heat it started with; 8s cooldown. The shield thrusts out and back, and a translucent fan on the ground shows exactly the cone it covered. Area of Effect deepens the cone; Damage scales the hit.
- **Vent (built).** Drains 10 heat/s and keeps going — past equilibrium and on to −100 — until it hits the floor or the Tank presses 3 again, with armor returning as heat falls. Heat gained mid-vent (hits, Slam, Retaliation's blast) is simply drained along with the rest. It needs heat above 0 to start, so cancelling below equilibrium means no restart until something heats him back up. Not a stance: he can move, swing and block throughout. 4s cooldown, from when it ends.
  - **Burn.** Every 0.5s, each enemy within 5m (measured flat) gets a small Burn: 0.125× the sword's attack damage over 1.5s. Burns merge, so an enemy standing in range settles at a constant 0.25× attack damage per second within about 2s however much heat is left, and one that walks out keeps burning briefly. Burn isn't a Fire Status, so Vent takes nothing from the Rituals — but it does set up Immolate. A merged Burn is credited to whoever applied it last, so a Vent washing over Ritual-lit Burns takes their kills.
  - **Visual:** a faint orange disc around the Tank with a crisp, near-opaque ring at the edge, so it reads apart from Retaliation's circle when both are down. It rides along as the Tank's child, so it lifts off the ground when he jumps — fine for a placeholder.
  - **Ability mods:** Area grows the radius, Damage the Burn. Duration stretches the gap between applications, so it's a straight penalty (a weaker Burn) until the planned mod-inverting currency exists and a negative Duration can speed it up instead.
- **Immolate (built).** The finisher that spends the dial. Needs heat above 0 to start; channels for 1s, then every enemy within 12m (measured flat) carrying a Fire Status or a Burn erupts. Going off takes _all_ his heat and drops him to −100 — full armor — and eruption damage scales with the heat it took, measured the moment it goes off, so heat gained or vented mid-channel counts. Who erupts is decided when the channel ends, so an enemy set alight during it still counts. The cooldown starts at the eruption. A personal, fire-only reaction; Detonate stays reserved for an all-element version over a larger area.
  - **It's a stance.** Feet planted, no swing, block or other ability. A Vent already running keeps going, and ends itself when Immolate drops heat to the floor. Pressing 4 again cancels for free — no heat spent, no cooldown — and so do dying and the wave ending.
  - **Eruption.** Each erupting enemy deals 1× the sword's attack damage × heat/100 as Fire to everything within 3m of it, itself included — flat, no crit roll, and it sets no Status, so eruptions can't chain. Eruptions overlap: an enemy in a packed, lit crowd takes one from every lit neighbor. That's the reward for gathering with Retaliation first, and the first thing to cap per enemy if it proves too strong. Each eruption goes off from where its enemy stood even if a neighbor's eruption killed it first; kills launch outward.
  - **Immolation** is the ailment Immolate leaves behind. Each erupting enemy's Fire Status and Burn are consumed and replaced with one — Fire Status alone is enough. Its base damage is that enemy's own eruption plus whatever the consumed Burn had left; overlapping eruptions are bonus damage and don't feed it. The base is paced as if dealt over 2s, ticking every 0.5s, and keeps ticking at that size for 4s — twice the base in total. Immolation alone doesn't make an enemy erupt, and a new one replaces an existing one outright rather than merging. It's credited to the Tank, including the share that came from a Ritual's Burn.
  - **Target visual (not built):** he drops his sword and shield, looks upward and shouts, arms out to the sides with palms up and fingers half-clenched — not quite fists — while his eyes turn a bright, glowing orange with energy pouring out of them. At the end of the channel every marked enemy detonates, then he picks the sword and shield back up.
  - **Placeholder visual:** the sword and shield hide for the channel; a thin red ring closes in from the edge of the radius onto the Tank, a flame-colored disc sits under every enemy that would erupt right now, and each eruption plays the Fireball's explosion sphere at the eruption radius.
  - **Ability mods:** Area grows both the 12m radius and each eruption. Damage scales the eruption, and through it the eruption's share of Immolation's base — not the consumed Burn's share, which came from someone else's gear. Duration stretches Immolation's 4s at the same tick size, so it pays off only at each 0.5s breakpoint (+12.5% per extra tick).
- **The intended rotation:** taunt to gather (the blast sets Fire Status on everything it hits), swing and Vent to keep them lit, Immolate while they're packed. Immolate lands him at full armor either way. **Open:** Vent drains the same heat Immolate turns into damage, so venting before an Immolate trades damage for armor — whether that's an interesting choice or just a trap is unknown until it's played.

### Architect

A distinct, non-combat role — places and preps towers, pre-crafts tokens, then isn't part of the fighting roster (in-fiction, he's sustaining the shell). He has **no equipment of his own**: `CharacterId` has no `Architect` value at all, so no item can target him even by mistake, and he has no health value since nothing can attack him during Build phase. Opening the inventory as the Architect shows the current Sentinel's loadout.

The player can **repair** towers mid-match by funneling mana into them, but cannot **reposition** them once placed — tokens cover "I need a tower somewhere else, right now."

**Not designed yet:** the player's own power/progression system, which is decided to share no mechanics with Runes. Character set bonuses are a far-future itemization idea.

**Stretch goal, not committed:** a real Portal-style gun that redirects enemies, not just a player teleport. The rendering is a known Godot technique (`SubViewport` + second camera + oblique near-clip plane); the real cost is that enemies path via `NavigationAgent3D`, which has no concept of falling through a portal and re-pathing from a landing spot. Worth an isolated prototype before ever attempting it for real.

### Controls

Keyboard and mouse and a controller are both first-class; tested on a PS4 pad. Every input goes through a named Input Map action carrying both bindings, so nothing in gameplay code names a key or button. `InputDevice` tracks whichever kind of device the player touched last (small stick drift and mouse bumps don't count), and the rest adapts to it: button prompts, the cursor, and whether the left stick drives the character or an open menu.

**Controller layout (field):**

| Input                                   | Action                                                                                                        |
| --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Left stick / Right stick                | Move / look (squared response curve, `StickLookSpeedDegrees` at full tilt)                                    |
| L3                                      | Sprint                                                                                                        |
| R2 / L2                                 | Attack and confirm placement / alt action (aim, block) and cancel placement                                   |
| Cross                                   | Jump                                                                                                          |
| Circle                                  | Dodge; cancel (`ui_cancel`) — backs out of token pick mode, a placement, or an ability waiting for its target |
| Square                                  | Interact / pick up                                                                                            |
| L1 + Cross / Circle / Square / Triangle | Abilities 1–4                                                                                                 |
| Hold R1 + d-pad left / up / right       | Tokens 1–3                                                                                                    |
| D-pad up / down (hold)                  | Repair / destroy the Ritual in view                                                                           |
| D-pad right                             | Ready up                                                                                                      |
| Triangle                                | Build menu (Architect)                                                                                        |
| Touchpad / Share / Options              | Inventory / character sheet / pause                                                                           |

- **Chords live in code, not the Input Map**, which can't require two buttons at once. `InputChords` gates them: while L1 is held the face buttons cast abilities and jump, dodge, interact and cancel stand aside; while R1 is held, repair, destroy and ready stand aside, since the d-pad belongs to tokens. A keyboard never holds a modifier, so its presses always pass straight through. Unmodified d-pad left is kept free for later (map pings and the like).
- **Holding R1 _is_ pick mode** on a controller — pressing it enters, releasing exits — where the keyboard's `Q` toggles.
- **Menus:** Cross confirms, Circle backs out, the left stick or d-pad moves focus, and a held direction repeats after 0.3s, every 0.1s (`UiNavigationRepeat` — nothing repeats controller input on its own). Wherever a layout is too sparse for Godot's automatic nearest-control pick, focus neighbors are set explicitly: the inventory's paper doll and the edge between the inventory and a crafting or vendor panel beside it. **On a controller, movement stops while any panel is open**, since the left stick is navigating it; keyboard players keep walking.
- **Mouse:** captured during play, released while any panel is open — and hidden instead on a controller, where a cursor parked wherever the mouse was left is only clutter. Escape never releases the mouse on its own.
- **Button prompts** name the binding for the current device (`InputPromptHelper`) and update the moment the player switches. A controller reads as PlayStation or Xbox from its reported name — a Switch pad reads as Xbox for now. The PlayStation face shapes and d-pad arrows are drawn (`ButtonGlyph`) rather than typed, since font coverage of those symbols varies. The field HUD's token and ability rows carry a modifier chip at their left (R1 or `Q` for tokens, L1 for abilities, controller only); on a controller both rows show their slot badges all the time, while on keyboard only one row does, since they share `1`/`2`/`3`.

**Pause menu** — Options, or Escape when nothing else wanted it: Resume / Options / Return to Hub (in a match only — a forfeit, carried out by the level's `GameOverHandler`) / Exit Game. Opening it closes any other open panel first. It freezes the game unless `PlayerProfiles.IsMultiplayer` — menus are per player, so in co-op, split-screen included, each player opens their own without stopping anyone else's game, which is also why it lives in the HUD rather than an autoload.

**Options** holds the display toggles (Section 8), saved per machine.

**Not built:** rebinding, stick sensitivity and invert settings, aim assist, rumble, button-icon art, the affix detail popup on a controller, and an on-screen keyboard (Steam's or a console's) for naming favorites. **Local split-screen** would need each controller assigned to a player seat — today every binding and `InputDevice` treat all devices as one player.

## 4. Rituals, Spells & Runes

A base skill (**Spell**, e.g. Fireball) is an immutable data definition. Modifiers (**Runes**) transform that definition or hook into its events. Composition, not subclassing.

**Tags** (Projectile, AoE, Duration, Trigger, elemental types) let a Rune apply generically to "anything with tag X" rather than needing per-spell logic. Elemental tags exist for build-crafting depth only — enemies have no elemental resistances by design (Section 5).

Elements so far: **Fire, Lightning, Cold, Wind, Water.** Runes socket into 4 slots per tower, one per corner. Towers don't level or scale within a match, and duplicate rune types on one Spell are rejected in code.

**Rune roster and naming.** Every rune's display name is one evocative word rather than a mechanical description, the way ARPGs typically name modifiers: **Haste** (cast speed), **Extend** (range), **Velocity** (projectile speed), **Salvo** (multi-projectile), **Expand** (increased area), plus **Chain**, **Fork**, **Pierce**, **Nova**, **Return**, and the **Ignition** reaction rune.

**Mana/capacity cost is multiplicative, PoE-support-style.** Every `Rune` has a `ManaCostMultiplier` applied automatically during resolve, multiplying the tower's resolved `ManaCost` in the same `base * (1 + increased) * more` shape as Damage/Range. Every rune currently uses a flat placeholder 1.2× for testing the mechanism; `MaxCapacity` is a placeholder 1000 to leave room to experiment before real numbers are chosen.

**Targeting.** `TargetPriority` is a flat field on `SpellCastContext`, seeded from `Spell.BaseTargetPriority` and read by `EnemyTargeting.FindTarget` — so a future targeting rune is an ordinary `Rune` writing one field, with no pipeline change. The default is `ClosestToGoal`, ranked by remaining nav-path length, which is comparable across lanes because every lane converges on the same objective. Range is always measured from the ritual; only the choice among in-range enemies varies. Chain jumps, forks and projectile redirects deliberately stay nearest-first, matching what those runes promise. Only `ClosestToGoal`/`ClosestToRitual` exist so far. **Player presence is never a targeting fallback** — multiple lanes mean the player can only be in one place.

**Interaction model.** Placement and aiming use a center-screen reticle plus a raycast into the world, not a screen-space cursor. This keeps mouse and controller unified under one system; full controller support is a requirement, not an afterthought (Section 3, Controls).

Player-buff towers (a "Buff Totem") buff the player instead of attacking, range-based so the player must stand near it, and cost capacity like anything else.

**Reading a Ritual at a glance (planned).** The rune circle on the ground around every Ritual lights up around it toward its next cast, so readiness reads from a distance for fast and cooldown-heavy towers alike, and dulls while recovering — a plain ring until the art pass. Holding Alt (`advanced_tooltip`) while looking at a Ritual shows its range circle, the same one placement shows.

**Line of sight.** Only terrain blocks it — Ice Walls, Rituals, players and enemies never do (`LineOfSight`, one ray against the World layer).

- **Rituals** only target what they can see from their spawn point; range is still measured from the base. Sight is checked last, and only for a candidate that would win, so a search costs few raycasts. Every chain and fork jump needs sight from the enemy it leaves. Bombardments need it too — a targeting rune that picks a spot could lift that later.
- **Soldier's rifle and Overdrive** stop at terrain and flash there; Overdrive's pierce ends at a wall.
- **Ranged enemies** don't fire without a clear shot: they close in along their path until they have one, and their projectiles stop on terrain.
- **Not covered:** melee and radius effects (explosions, Immolate, fields) still reach through terrain.

**Visual direction.** Every tower is fundamentally magical, so the physical shape needn't change per spell. The base is a floating structure with a central energy core; the spell determines the energy color, the central rune's shape, the crystal geometry, the orbiting symbols, the rune circle's behavior and the particles. A fire spell gets angular rapidly-rotating fragments and a turbulent core; an ice spell gets crystalline structures and slow orbiting runes.

### Rune acquisition

Runes carry the same `Rarity` axis Items use. **Common is unlocked for every rune from the start** so a new player always has real options; higher tiers must be found. Storage is one `.tres` per rarity tier per rune type — accepted as authoring overhead rather than re-plumbing `SpellInstance`/`Ritual.RuneSlots`/`BuildMenu`/`RitualPlacer` to carry a tier dimension everywhere a bare `Rune` reference currently flows. Each type gets a subfolder under `assets/runes/`, and `RuneCatalog` scans recursively.

- **Drops** come through `LootGenerator.GenerateRandomDrop()` like gear, but roll `Rarity` from their own steeper table — Uncommon 100, Rare 30, Epic 8, Legendary 1, no Common since it's always known — since a tier is a permanent unlock rather than one item. Then pick uniformly among that rarity's not-yet-known runes. A rarity with nothing left to teach is excluded from the roll; if everything is known, it falls back to a gear roll rather than wasting itself.
- **A drop is a `RuneTome : Item`**, reusing world-item pickup, nameplates and tooltips entirely. It sits in Inventory until the player right-clicks it (`inventory_use`, Square on a controller) to learn it.
- **Cascade-unlock:** learning a higher tier grants every lower not-yet-known tier of the same rune too, so owned tiers are always a contiguous range from Common upward. This is what keeps the tier-picker UI simple — nothing to grey out, only ever list what's owned.
- **Tier values.** Each tier steps one number: a bonus grows (Expand, Velocity and Extend 25% → 45% in 5% steps; Haste 15% → 35%; Chill's slow 25% → 37% in 3% steps; Pierce 2 → 6), or a damage penalty shrinks by 0.05 per tier without ever reaching zero (Salvo and Chain 0.75 → 0.95, Fork and Return 0.55 → 0.75, Nova 0.35 → 0.55). Reaction runes get about 5% stronger per tier (Ignition's Burn 50% → 60% of the hit). Legendary is roughly 1.5–2× a Common's bonus; Common bases sit a step low so higher tiers have room. **Shroud is Common-only** — it's on or off, with nothing to scale. **A new rune ships with all its tiers.**

**Per-copy scarcity (framework only).** Independent of rarity, a known rune can be limited-quantity (`RuneInventoryEntry.Unlimited = false`). `Quantity` is the total ever owned and is never decremented; "available right now" (`RuneCatalog.IsAvailable`) is derived live by scanning currently-placed Rituals' `RuneSlots`. This matters: an earlier spend-and-refund counter was scrapped because any path skipping `Ritual.Destroy` — a crash included — permanently lost a copy. Deriving availability can't desync. Nothing currently grants a limited entry, so this is inert. **Tokens can never use a limited rune**, since a token's loadout locks in during the unpressured Planning phase, exactly when a scarce rune should go on a real tower instead. Favorites may reference one and simply show unavailable when out of copies.

### Ice Wall

A destructible blockade that physically blocks a lane — enemies that reach it attack it directly rather than routing around, and it blocks the player too. It also expires after a duration cap if never broken, so it can't wall off a lane forever unattended. Either way, the casting Ritual goes on cooldown before recasting; a wall reappearing later is a fresh `IceWall`, not the original reforming.

Implemented as a real `Spell` (`IceWallSpell`) so it goes through the existing placement/economy/rune pipeline, with `WallCastEffect` as a third `CastEffect` alongside Projectile and InstantHit. Placement is three stages — wall start, wall end, then a linked tower within `Range` of the wall's line. Destroying the tower frees the wall, since the wall is parented under the tower's spawn point. Each prospective segment position is validated against the `Obstacles` layer, and the whole preview flips red if any segment is invalid. The wall auto-segments between its two points (minimum 2) so a token-sized wall can't fully block a lane.

- **Cost: the base covers the shortest wall, then each extra segment adds its own cost** — 15 for two segments, 25 for three, and so on, all scaled by the usual cost multipliers (`IceWall.ChargedSegmentCount`). A wall can't be shorter than two segments, so the shortest one costs exactly the spell's listed base.
- **All of this lives with the spell, not in the generic placement code.** `IceWallSpell.CreatePlacementFlow` returns a `WallPlacementFlow`, which owns the endpoint stages, per-segment validation, the zone the tower must sit in, and the footprint marker. `RitualPlacer` knows nothing about walls. The picked endpoints become `WallPlacementData`, which the tower keeps and which folds into every resolve of its context — the endpoints onto `WallCastData`, the charged segments into its cost — so the cast, the trigger and the cost paid all read the same wall.

- **Health is one shared pool**, not per-segment: `IceWall` owns segment health × count, any segment's damage drains it, and every segment breaks together.
- **`Expired` and `Destroyed` are two separate signals**, kept distinct so a future "explode on destruction" rune can react to one and not the other.
- **Casting is gated by a `CastTrigger` hook** rather than the usual nearest-enemy check. `WallProximityCastTrigger` waits for an enemy within range of _any point along the wall's line_ (read from `context.Wall`), so a long wall spanning lanes still triggers. The `CastTrigger` base is generic, so a future "if an enemy steps here" rune can add its own trigger; this particular one is wall-specific.
- **Cooldown doesn't start until the wall is gone** — a general mechanism, not wall-specific. `CastEffect` has an `IsOngoing` flag, and an ongoing effect calls `Ritual.ConcludeOngoingEffect()` once whatever it left behind is gone; `SpellInstance` has `BeginOngoingEffect()`/`EndOngoingEffect(cooldown)` folded into `CanCast`. `Ritual` only talks to the abstract surface and has no knowledge of `IceWall`.
- **Enemies commit to a wall** within a detection radius, path to whichever point on the wall's line is nearest their own position (not a shared center, so converging enemies don't all aim at one spot), and attack the nearest segment once in range.
- **Do not try rebaking the navmesh around an active wall.** It was tried and fully reverted: any valid detour means pathfinding walks everyone around the wall, defeating its entire purpose, and recomputing shortest paths as walls appear and vanish has no concept of "already committed to this route," so paths oscillate. Walls are pure physical blockers.
- **Known rough edge:** enemies packed densely at a wall's face visibly jitter, from many overlapping `CharacterBody3D`s plus `NavigationAgent3D` avoidance fighting over the same overlaps. Disabling enemy-vs-enemy collision was rejected — it would let enemies overlap freely and make AoE hit far more targets than intended. The likely fix is decoupling simulation position (used for AoE and targeting) from rendered position, smoothed toward it so a hard physics correction doesn't pop.

A rune that makes the wall explode on break is planned.

### Ice Storm

Cold's first damage spell, and the one that makes Chill reachable. The Ritual calls hail down on its current target: damage lands across an area over a short window rather than in one instant, and a patch of `ChilledGround` is left behind that slows anything crossing it long after the hail stops.

- **Two independent things, spawned side by side.** `BombardmentCastEffect` drops a `Bombardment` and a `GroundEffect` at the same point; neither knows the other exists. That's what lets a rune leave chilled ground off something that isn't a bombardment at all, and lets a bombardment exist without leaving anything.
- **Meteor Shower is the same class with different data.** `Bombardment` applies no ailment of its own and carries no element, so a second spell of this shape costs a `Spell` subclass, a `.tres` and a visual scene — no systems code.
- **Damage is per second, not per cast.** Ticks land on each `TickInterval` mark that falls inside the storm's duration (0.2, 0.4, 0.6 for a 0.75s storm — a partial interval at the end earns nothing), each dealing damage-per-second × interval. So a longer duration means more total damage, the way PoE treats duration on damage over time, while the tick cadence — authored on the bombardment's scene beside the visual it drives — changes the rhythm, never the damage per second.
- **The storm slows nothing.** Being caught in it is worse than crossing the leftover ground because of the damage, not a bigger slow. An ailment on the bombardment would be Ice-Storm-specific and a meteor shower would want nothing to do with it.
- **`ChillRune` stacks with the ground rather than competing.** `ChillAilment` and `ChilledGroundAilment` are separate types, so they take separate slots and multiply: 30% and 25% resolve to a 47.5% slow inside a rune-socketed storm, decaying to 25% once only the frozen ground remains.
- **Targeting is the spell default** (`ClosestToGoal`), which aims at the enemy furthest along the path — the front edge of a pack, and so the fewest enemies. A "most enemies nearby" priority is the natural one for any bombardment and is a single enum field on the context; deferred until there are more priorities to add at once.

### Damage & repair

**A Ritual sits on two physics layers, and the split is load-bearing.** `Rituals` means _blocks movement_ — it's what enemies and the player collide with. `RitualHitbox` means _everything else a Ritual's body is for_: being shot by enemy projectiles, being raycast by repair and destroy, and blocking placement of another tower on the same spot. They were one layer until the **Shroud** rune needed a tower that enemies walk through but can still be hit and repaired, which is impossible with a single bit — clearing it takes the hits and the repairs with it. Shroud clears `Rituals` alone. Consequence to remember: a mask that wants "is there a tower here" wants `RitualHitbox`, and only movement collision should ever reference `Rituals`.

A damaged tower is normally **disabled, not destroyed**, and the active Sentinel repairs it by funneling mana in. True destruction is rare and returns attached runes to inventory, never losing them. The **Sapper** is the enemy that meaningfully threatens towers — a visible, prioritizable threat rather than universal collateral risk. Disabled towers show on the minimap and their core mesh turns gray.

Repair and destroy both use the same reticle raycast as placement, capped to a similar interaction range, with a contextual world-space prompt near the tower. **Repair and destroy are both hold-to-confirm (~1s)**, each with a progress indicator on the same prompt, cancelling if released early or if the raycast loses the tower. Repair's cost shows on its own line under the prompt, tinted red when unaffordable, and only while the tower is actually damaged. Chosen over a persistent mode toggle specifically to avoid mode-confusion on the harder-to-reverse action.

**Repair and refund pricing share one rule: the missing-health fraction times `GameRules.RepairCostFraction` (0.5).** A full repair of a broken tower costs half the tower's _own_ cost at its owner's current multipliers — placement extras like an Ice Wall's segments are left out, since the tower's health doesn't scale with them. Destroying refunds the mana actually paid, scaled down by the same fraction: everything back at full health, half back when broken, rounded down. So tearing down a broken tower to rebuild it is never a way around repairing it. Capacity always comes back in full — it's a ceiling on what can be built, not a currency, and shorting it would lose it for the rest of the match.

### Construction & deployment UI

`Q` drives two flows that behave nothing alike, built as separate systems because they sit under opposite time pressure. On a controller they split across two buttons: Triangle for the construction menu, R1 for tokens.

**Architect, Build phase — construction menu.** `Q` (Triangle) opens `BuildMenu`, a full-screen radial menu (design below): an element ring, then that element's spells, then the Ritual Inscription where runes are socketed, then placement. No time pressure, so this is a rich browsing experience.

- **Built so far:** the element ring (Favorites at the top, then Fire, Lightning, Cold, Water, Wind, Physical and Multi-Element clockwise), each element's spell ring, each spell's favorites ring, the pinned favorites wheel, the Ritual Inscription with its rune bucket ring, rune ring and tier wheel, Save Favorite, token authoring through the token wheel, and the spiral for rings past 8 items. On the wheels the center shows the highlighted item's name and its cost against current mana, red when unaffordable; the description sits below the wheel on its own dark panel, hidden when empty.
- **Layout.** No dimmed backdrop — the world stays visible. The wheel sits at a fixed anchor up and left of screen center (40% across, 42% down, to be tuned) for mouse and controller alike, keeping the reticle, and the spot about to be built on, uncovered. It can't open at the cursor, since the mouse is captured during play and has no position of its own. Text over the world or over wedge colors (which run from dark to near-white) carries a black outline. Circle, Escape or right-click steps back a ring, and closes the menu from the element ring; `Q` (Triangle), the key that opened it, closes it from any ring.
- **Not yet available:** the Arrange screen (deleting, renaming, reordering and pinning favorites by hand), sort modes and the which-wheel-opens-first setting.
- **Socketing rules:** socket state survives backing out and returning for the same spell, even across closing the menu, clearing only when a different spell is picked. A rune that conflicts with one already socketed on the same spell — the same type, or a second reaction rune — or that's unavailable shows dimmed in place and can't be picked, making the rule visible instead of silently rejecting it. Deliberate tier downgrades are intended, e.g. socketing a Common damage rune to land on an exact breakpoint another tower can finish. Clearing a whole near-finished build takes a held press, so a stray press can't wipe it. Rune description text lives on the `Rune` resource, not in UI code.
- Renders as a blocking overlay over the opening player's view. Since co-op has both players building simultaneously, a true game pause won't work — the overlay needs per-player scoping, mechanism still open.

**Favorites.** A saved spell plus up to 4 exact-tier `Rune` references. **Saved instantly under a default name**, with no naming prompt: the rune with the highest cost multiplier, then the spell — "Shroud Fireball" — or just the spell's name with nothing socketed. Ties (every rune currently shares a placeholder 1.2×) go to the earliest socket; a hand-ordered rune priority list could replace the cost rule later. Collisions get a suffix, "Shroud Fireball (1)". Renaming belongs to the Arrange screen, so a controller player never needs text entry and the captured mouse never needs a dialog. **Saving an exact duplicate is refused** — same spell, same runes at the same tiers, in any sockets, since socket order doesn't change what a ritual does — and the inscription names the favorite that already holds it. Picking one goes straight to placement — it's a locked stamp, not a pre-filled starting point. Where favorites are saved and picked in the radial menu is in its design below.

**Tokens.** Up to 3 held unused at once — a "how many can sit ready" cap, not a per-wave cast limit. Crafting and recrafting are Build-phase-only and cost nothing up front; mana and capacity are spent only at deployment, specifically so a token shores up a defense that didn't quite get finished rather than becoming a second economy to budget during prep. Authoring happens in the construction menu (the token wheel, below), with a gold ring around every wheel and a gold "Token N" tag marking the menu the whole time, so it's never ambiguous that confirming writes to a token instead of starting placement.

- **The Architect reaches tokens directly.** It has no abilities, so 1/2/3 on keyboard — or R1 with d-pad left/up/right, exactly as when deploying — open the build menu straight into authoring that token, from the field or from inside the menu. While the Architect is active the HUD's token row always shows its 1/2/3 badges and hides its keyboard `Q` chip (`Q` opens the Architect's build menu, not pick mode), and the ability row hides its keyboard badges. Nothing signals a switch between the Architect and a Sentinel, so both rows watch for it each frame.

**Any player, mid-wave — token deployment.** `Q` toggles a lightweight pick mode that remaps `1`/`2`/`3` to the player's tokens. Right-click, `Q`, or Escape all back out. On a controller, pick mode lasts exactly as long as R1 is held, with d-pad left/up/right as the three tokens. Pressing a mapped number drops into the same ghost/reticle placement a real tower uses; confirming spends live and consumes the token, cancelling doesn't. No browsing, no sockets — optimized for speed under pressure. Tokens are consumable: destroying a tower built from one doesn't give it back.

The field HUD (`AbilityHud`, holding `TokenBar` and `AbilityBar` as independent siblings rather than one combined class) is two permanent rows — 3 token slots colored by spell element and labeled, above 4 ability slots showing each ability's name and a cooldown-fraction fill. Each slot's badge names its binding on the current device, and each row has a modifier chip at its left (Section 3, Controls). On keyboard only one row shows badges at a time, since they share the `1`/`2`/`3` keys: abilities normally, tokens while pick mode is active — each row decides its own badge visibility off the same `TokenPickModeChanged` signal, with no shared state between them. On a controller the two rows' buttons never overlap, so both always show.

**Crosshair cues (`CrosshairCue`).** An ability can show state around the crosshair, read from `Ability` each frame so any Sentinel can opt in. A circle 5× the crosshair's size stays empty; everything sits outside it.

- **Shots left** while an ability holds the attack binding: Health Link's two arcs flank the crosshair and go black left then right as spikes are fired; Overdrive's shots are a row of bars below it in the dominant element's color, going black from the right.
- **Time bars** below that, one row per ability that has one, in fixed order so a bar's position says whose it is: Weak-Point Visor's duration, Health Link's arming window, Overdrive's expiry.

**Navigation is gamepad-first**, not mouse-first-then-retrofit.

### Radial construction menu (mostly built)

**The wheel itself is built:** `RadialWheel` (`ui/radial/`) is a reusable Control that draws a ring of wedges, turns a direction into a highlighted slot and commits it, knowing nothing about what its items are. The left stick reaches it through the `radial_left`/`radial_right`/`radial_up`/`radial_down` actions — its own actions rather than `move_*`, which includes WASD and would walk a keyboard player's character — so rebinding it to the right stick later is only an Input Map change. The mouse can't be bound to an action, so it has its own path into the same pointing code. A panel pushed with `UiPanelStack.Push(..., capturesMouse: true)` keeps the mouse captured, and `PlayerCharacter.HandleLook` ignores mouse motion while any panel is open, so pointing at a wedge never turns the camera. Commit is on press: an empty or disabled wedge can be highlighted but not committed. Because a press can start placement, `RitualPlacer` won't let a `confirm_placement` press that was already held when placement began confirm it — it polls input rather than receiving events, so marking the click handled wouldn't stop it. Backing out belongs to the host, which knows whether that means the previous ring or closing. Two switches let a host take over parts of it: `DrawWedges` off leaves only the pointing (the host draws its own controls), and `HandlesCommit` off leaves presses to the host (for telling a tap from a hold). `ReplaceItems` swaps a wheel's items without resetting where it's pointing, for an item that changes mid-gesture. Given more than 8 items, a `RadialWheel` becomes a spiral (below) on its own, reporting item indexes rather than wedges, so hosts don't change. `BuildMenu` hosts all the wheels and `RitualInscription`; the rest of this section is the design they're being built toward.

**Two kinds of wheel.** _Wedge wheels_ are plain `RadialWheel`s — the element, spell, favorites and rune rings and the tier wheel — and commit on press. _Button wheels_ derive from `ButtonWheel`, which wraps a `RadialWheel` with wedges and commit both off and adds the layer on top: buttons placed around a center and lit when pointed at, tap versus hold (with a fill on rectangular buttons and a progress ring just outside round ones), a pointer dot, an optional backdrop circle with outlined wedges showing each slot's share of the pointing, center text, fading all but one slot, and relabeling a slot temporarily. A subclass supplies only its layout — slot count, each slot's position, size, shape and pointing weight — so the geometry lives with it; an empty slot still takes its weight, so a weight of 0 leaves a direction unreachable. `RitualInscription` and `TokenWheel` are the two so far.

Replaces the construction menu's tile grids — spell grid, Favorites tab, rune picker and tier popup — with radial wheels. The grids get clunky as the spell, rune and favorite counts scale, and a wheel makes picking a build a physical motion: the goal is a **marking menu**, where an experienced player stops reading the wheel and just performs the gesture ("up, then right" is Chain Lightning), which is also what gives building a tower the feel of casting a spell. The Token deployment flow (pick mode mid-wave) is untouched.

**Positions never move unless the player moves them.** Muscle memory only forms if an item is always in the same direction, so almost every rule below follows from this one:

- A spell's slot is fixed, not packed alphabetically — learning a new spell never shifts the others.
- **Unlearned spells show as locked slots** in their fixed position. Undecided against the alternative of the pie gaining slices as spells are learned; the fixed 8-position geometry below keeps positions stable either way.
- The element ring is built with its **Physical** and **multi-element** wedges from the start, dimmed until a spell exists for them, so the first one arriving doesn't rotate every element's angle.

**Structure.**

- **Every wheel aims for 8 wedges.** Eight puts every wedge on a cardinal or a diagonal — the easiest directions to hit reliably on a stick — and means a direction is the same place on every wheel.
- **Spell wheel.** Ring 1 is 8 wedges: **Favorites** at the top (the pinned favorites wheel, below — the most-used thing gets the most prominent direction), then clockwise Fire, Lightning, Cold, Water, Wind, Physical, and a rainbow **multi-element** wedge. Ring 2 is that element's spells in their slots. Ice Wall is Cold. The Favorites wedge is meant to carry a star icon once wedges can draw icons; it's a text label until then, since font coverage of symbols varies.
  - **A spell's wedge is worked out from its tags**, not authored: one element tag puts it under that element, none puts it under Physical, and more than one (Cataclysm) puts it under multi-element. Each spell has exactly one home, which keeps custom ordering simple. Listing a multi-element spell at the end of each of its elements instead is the alternative if players expect to find it there.
  - **Default order is an authored number on each `Spell`**, currently the order spells were added in development, to be revisited once there's a real unlock order. The Acquired sort needs nothing new: `KnownSpells` is appended to as spells are learned, so it's already in acquisition order.
- **Favorites are nested under their spell**, not in a separate wheel: element → spell → [New build | Favorite A | Favorite B …]. A favorite is a spell plus runes, so it lives with its spell; the ring under any one spell stays small; and "my Fireball chain build" becomes a three-flick incantation starting with the same motion as plain Fireball. A separate Favorites wheel filtered by element was considered and dropped — it bunches every Fire favorite into one ring, which overflows fast.
  - **New build** is the top slot; favorites follow in saved order, so a new one never moves the others. Picking a favorite goes straight to placement; one with a currently unavailable rune is dimmed. Backing out of the inscription lands on New build.
  - **A spell with no favorites skips the ring** and goes straight to a new build, for now. The cost is that the gesture for a new build of that spell gains a flick once its first favorite is saved; always showing the ring, with New build alone at the top, is the alternative if that proves jarring.
- **Pinned favorites wheel.** One static 8-slot wheel of favorites, for truly fast access to the towers a player uses most. It never spirals, and every favorite on it is still reachable under its own spell too — pinning only adds a shortcut.
  - **It fills itself automatically:** the player's first 8 favorites land in its slots as they're created, and on load any favorites not yet pinned backfill free slots in saved order — which is also how favorites saved before the wheel existed get onto it. The element ring's Favorites wedge is dimmed until something is pinned. From there the player customizes it — pinning, unpinning and choosing which slot each pin sits in, since the direction is the whole point. There's no sorting on this wheel, and an empty slot stays empty rather than collapsing. Deleting a favorite clears its pin.
  - **Auto-fill stops for good once the player takes charge of the wheel** — one flag per profile. Taking charge means deciding what a slot holds: pinning a favorite into an empty slot, or unpinning one. Once a player has said "I want to be in charge of where things go," the wheel is theirs and new favorites no longer land on it on their own. Swapping filled slots doesn't count, so rearranging auto-filled favorites keeps auto-fill running; neither does deleting a favorite, which empties its slot for the next new favorite to fill. Tracking this as a single flag was chosen over marking individual slots as claimed, since it's simpler to explain to players.
  - **Reached through the element ring's Favorites wedge**, so it needs no binding of its own and a pinned favorite is two flicks away, like any spell. The spell wheel opens by default; a setting makes the pinned wheel open first instead, for one-flick access, with backing out of it landing on the element ring. The default is a fixed rule or the setting, never dependent on whether anything is pinned.
  - Selecting a pin behaves like any favorite: straight to placement, or stamping the token slot while authoring a token. Affordability shows the same way as everywhere else.
  - **Saved as a fixed 8-entry list of favorite names**, not the favorites themselves. A favorite is a tagged class written out in full wherever it appears, so a pin holding one would save a second copy that loads as a separate object and drifts from the original. Names are already unique (collisions are auto-suffixed). It's `PlayerSaveData.PinnedFavorites`, padded to 8 on load like Tokens; an empty slot is an empty string rather than a missing entry, so the load-time gap removal never collapses it. A pin naming no current favorite shows as empty. `PinnedWheelCustomized` is the taken-charge flag.
- **Ritual Inscription** (`RitualInscription`) — where runes are inscribed onto a spell, the step that makes it a complete ritual. Picking a spell (or New build) opens it: the spell at the center, the 4 sockets on the diagonals, and the build's actions on the cardinals. It's a `ButtonWheel`, so it's picked by direction exactly like a wedge wheel, over the same 8 directions, but **it doesn't look like one**: sockets are 182px circles and actions are buttons in an ordinary layout, lit up with a white border as they're pointed at. Unlike the other wheels it sits at screen center, on a dark ~1070px circle behind its controls rather than a full-screen dim; the circle is what to replace when there's art for it. Sizes are in the project's 2560×1440 base resolution, which Godot scales to the real one (`canvas_items` stretch, `expand` aspect), so the menu's layout has that whole space to work with at any resolution.
  - **Sockets are easier to point at than actions.** A wheel item can carry a weight — its share of the angle relative to its neighbors — and sockets weigh twice an action, so each socket answers to 60° of pointing and each action 30°. Every item stays centered on its compass direction; weight only moves the boundaries between neighbors. Diagonals are the hardest directions to land on a stick, and sockets are what gets picked most.
  - **A pointer dot** shows where the stick or mouse points, semi-transparent and drawn behind the center text, since the inscription hides its wheel's wedges and with them the wheel's own dot.
  - **Actions on the cardinals rather than face buttons**, so a keyboard-and-mouse player can do everything by pointing and clicking, and no confirmation popup is needed (a popup would want a visible cursor, which a captured mouse doesn't have). Placed where they feel natural on a controller, echoing the PlayStation face buttons' positions: **bottom Place** (confirm the build and go to placement), **right Clear All**, **top Save Favorite**, **left Save as Token**. They're directions only — Cross still presses whatever is highlighted, so the face buttons can't double as shortcuts without breaking that.
  - **It tells a tap from a hold**, reading presses itself rather than leaving them to its wheel. Holds are specific to the inscription; every other wheel stays commit-on-press, since those are meant to be as quick as possible. A slot with no hold time fires on press; one with a hold time fires its tap on release, or its hold once pressed that long, and pointing away mid-hold cancels.
    - **A filled socket:** tap swaps its rune (straight to the rune wheel), hold past the same 0.3s threshold ability slots use opens the tier wheel. An empty socket goes to the rune wheel on press.
    - **Clear All is hold-to-confirm for 1s**, like repair and destroy, with the button filling as it's held; a tap just says to hold. A socket's hold shows as a ring filling around the socket instead, since a filling rectangle inside a circle reads as a glitch.
  - **Save Favorite** saves instantly under a default name and says which (see Favorites above).
  - **Save as Token** (left) opens the token wheel to pick a slot, from an ordinary build. While authoring a token (below) the inscription changes instead: the bottom action becomes **Save Token**, and the left becomes **Clear Token**, held 1s to empty the token and enabled only when it holds one.
  - **Flow:** it opens with the first empty socket highlighted, or Place once every socket is full. After a rune is socketed it reopens with the next empty socket highlighted, so filling a build is one continuous chain. Backing out returns to the spell ring with that spell highlighted.
  - **The center** shows the spell's name and total cost against current mana, plus a line about the highlighted socket or action.
- **Rune wheel.** Ring 1 is a rune bucket, ring 2 the compatible runes in it, each at its highest owned tier, colored by rarity. Swapping a socket's rune opens it highlighting that rune if it's in the bucket picked. The buckets are temporary (below).
  - **It opens around the socket being filled, drawn over the inscription**: the socket itself is the ring's hole (plus a small gap so its highlight border shows), with wedges about as thick as the spell wheel's. The bucket ring, rune ring and tier wheel all open there, so inscribing reads as happening _at that spot_. The rest of the inscription fades while it's open, the socket's label previews the rune pointed at, and the inscription's center cost shows the total with it.
  - **The rune's name, cost change and description sit in a wide panel on the ring's side of the inscription** — above it for the top two sockets, below it for the bottom two — with name and cost on one row and the description under them, since the ring overlaps the inscription's center text. A description that runs long grows the panel away from the ring, never into it.
- **Tier wheel.** Holding a filled socket opens a small 8-slot wheel: **Swap** at the top (opens the rune wheel for this socket — likely the most common reason to open a filled socket), then clockwise from the top-right an arc of **Common, Uncommon, Rare, Epic, Legendary**, rarity rising clockwise, and **Clear** (unsocket) on the left. Tiers not owned are dimmed, never hidden, so positions never change. It opens with the socket's current tier highlighted. A bumper shortcut for stepping tiers inside the rune wheel was considered; the keyboard-and-mouse equivalent would collide with the scroll wheel (used for spiral laps, below), so it's left out unless picking a lower tier proves slow in play.
- **Backing out** steps one screen at a time: tier wheel or bucket ring → inscription, rune ring → bucket ring, inscription → spell ring. `Q` closes the whole menu from anywhere.
- **Later, maybe: flick-and-release.** For the quick wheels, moving the stick and releasing it could commit with no button press at all. Button-press commit comes first to see how it feels.

- **Token wheel** (`TokenWheel`, a `ButtonWheel`). Three round buttons at **left, top and right** — tokens 1, 2 and 3, matching the field's d-pad left/up/right and keys 1/2/3. It's never meant to hold 8, so it breaks the 8-wedge rule on purpose. Its four directions are weighted 2 / 1 / 0 / 1 (up, right, down, left), splitting the pointing into a top, right and left third, with down unreachable; a fourth token would give down a real weight. Each token's share of the pointing is drawn as an outlined wedge behind its button, always visible, and the pointed-at wedge lights up rather than the button. The field HUD's token row stays visible under the menu, so the wheel doesn't repeat it. The center shows the pointed-at token's runes and deploy cost.
  - **Reaching it:** R1 inside the menu opens it to pick a token to author; 1/2/3 (or R1 with the d-pad) skip it and go straight to that token. The inscription's Save as Token opens it to choose where the current build goes.
  - **Choosing where to save a build:** tapping an empty slot saves there; a filled slot needs a 1s hold to replace, and a tap just says so. Then it's back to the inscription, which can still place the build.
  - **Authoring a token** puts the menu in token mode: a gold ring around whichever wheel is up — outside the wedges of a wedge wheel, along the inscription's backdrop circle — says something is different, and a gold "Token N" tag says what. The tag sits at a fixed spot just left of the menu, the one place nothing else ever appears (the rune info panel is always above or below the inscription, and the rune rings stay inside its circle), so it never collides with another label. A filled token opens the inscription pre-filled with its build; an empty one starts at the element ring. Picking a favorite stamps the token with its build and name instead of placing it. Limited-copy runes, and favorites holding one, are dimmed, since a token may never hold a limited rune. Saving or clearing a token picked on the token wheel returns there with that slot highlighted; one reached straight from 1/2/3 (or R1 with the d-pad) closes the menu. A token reached straight from 1/2/3 closes the menu when backed out of the screen it opened on — the element ring for an empty token, the inscription for a filled one; otherwise backing out of the element ring in token mode returns to the token wheel; backing out of the token wheel lands on the ordinary element ring.
  - **A token is named after the favorite holding exactly its build**, if one exists, and otherwise shows its spell's name. Saving an edited favorite-stamped token unchanged keeps the name; editing it into a different build drops it rather than mislabeling it; a hand-built token matching a favorite picks the name up.
  - **Token authoring reuses the menu's in-progress build** — the selected spell and sockets — as the grid menu did, so editing a token and backing out leaves its build in the inscription.

**Save data.** Sort mode, custom orders, the pinned wheel, its taken-charge flag and the which-wheel-opens-first setting are all new `[Export]` fields on the profile. Adding fields needs no schema version or migration — old saves load them at their defaults (Section 7, Save format) — so none of this waits on save work.

**The center of the wheel** replaces the description panel: the hovered item's art in the background with its name, description and cost overlaid. Anything that won't fit goes on the outer edge. Costs are the **resolved** cost against current mana — what the player actually needs is whether they can afford it — with the rune wheel showing the cost change a rune would add. Wedges the player can't currently afford are dimmed or carry a red cost.

**Input.**

- **The stick picks the wedge, Cross commits.** The highlight stays on the last wedge when the stick returns to center, so the player can let go and then press. **Circle backs out** one level, and out of the menu from the top.
- **The mouse acts as a virtual stick:** mouse movement accumulates into a direction clamped to the wheel's radius, so it's the same gesture as the controller. That needs raw mouse movement, so **the mouse stays captured while a wheel is open** — a deliberate exception to the rule that the mouse is released while a panel is open (Section 3, Controls).
- **Flick-and-return** (a flick past a threshold commits as the stick springs back, no button press) is deferred until the wheel exists to try it on.
- Bindings in general aren't settled.

**Rings with more than 8 items become a spiral.** Past about 8 wedges, picking the right one with a stick gets unreliable, but a player with dozens of favorites under one spell still needs to reach them all without paging through menus.

- **There are always 8 fixed wedge positions**, and item _n_ always appears at wedge _n_ mod 8 — item 9 where item 1 was, item 10 where item 2 was. The direction a player learns for an item stays true on every lap.
- **The first lap is absolute**, so a ring of 8 or fewer items is pure flick-to-slot and never spirals.
- **Rotating continuously past the end of a lap scrolls to the next one.** The wedges don't move; their contents swap, fading from the outgoing item to the incoming one, so the list progresses without the wheel itself rotating.
- **The seam moves with the stick**: the break between laps always sits directly opposite it, the wedges ahead showing upcoming items and the ones behind showing what's been passed, fading by distance along the spiral. Chosen over a fixed seam between wedges 8 and 1, where wedge 1 would abruptly stop being item 1. A lap counter at the bottom of the center ("2 / 3") and a glowing line in the gap at the seam sell the spiral; the line marks any break between neighboring wedges, so it also shows where the list ends.
- **It stops at the last item** rather than wrapping. Rotating backward past item 1 also stops.
- **A flick lands on whatever the wedge is currently showing**, never jumping laps.
- **Returning the stick to center keeps the current lap**, the same way it keeps the highlight. Stepping back a lap means rotating backward — an accepted inconvenience, since spirals only exist for players with a lot of content.
- On a mouse, the virtual stick handles laps the same way, and the scroll wheel jumps a whole lap.

**Sort modes and custom order.** A sort mode is a saved preference, not a toggle — a player picks one and keeps it, which makes it just a different fixed layout and keeps muscle memory intact. The element ring never sorts; modes apply inside it (spells within an element, favorites within a spell, runes within a group).

- **Default** (authored order), **Alphabetical**, **Acquired** (order obtained), and **Custom**. Acquired is fully stable — new things always land at the end — while Alphabetical inserts mid-list and shifts what follows. Mana cost was considered and dropped: the base cost isn't what a player is weighing, and the resolved cost changes with gear.
- **Most Used (idea, not prioritized):** spells and runes ordered by how often this profile has placed them, most frequent first. Needs per-profile usage counts. It's the one mode that isn't a fixed layout — an item climbs past another as counts change — so when it reorders (live, or only between matches) is open.
- **Custom order is set on a separate Arrange screen**, not by rearranging wedges inside the wheel: element tabs, then a list of that element's spells to reorder, with favorites arranged under their spell. The order is per-profile save data.
- **Runes get a custom order too**, likely as **one global rune order** that every group shows in relative order — a rune is arranged once even if it appears in several groups.
- Where sort mode is changed from isn't decided. Not on a bare R3 press inside the wheel, since an accidental click mid-gesture would rearrange everything.

**Open.**

- **Rune buckets — temporary, subject to change.** The rune wheel's first ring is 8 fixed buckets, clockwise from the top: **Trajectory, Area, Duration, Cooldown, Reaction, Trigger, Tradeoff, Utility.** They're broad on purpose. An Element bucket was dropped because nearly every elemental rune will be a reaction rune anyway.
  - **A rune's buckets come from its own tags.** Runes gain `Rune.Tags` — what kind of rune it is — separate from `RequiredTags`/`RequiredAnyTags`, which stay the compatibility check against a spell. This is PoE's support-gem tagging: a tag can exist mostly for sorting and still give a later item or unique something to hook ("Tradeoff runes have 50% less downside").
  - **One shared `Tag` enum.** The rune-only values (Trajectory, Cooldown, Reaction, Tradeoff, Utility; Trigger and Duration already exist) join the same enum spells use, so a cross-cutting effect can name "Projectile" once for spells and runes alike. The Area bucket reuses `Tag.AoE` rather than adding a near-duplicate. The compiler can't stop a spell carrying a rune-only tag or a rune requiring one — which would make that rune incompatible with every spell and silently absent — so a startup check throws on both.
  - **A rune can carry several bucket tags** and then shows in each of those wedges. It must carry at least one, or it would appear nowhere; the same startup check enforces that. Organizing a much larger roster better is a problem for when it exists.
  - **Compatibility still filters inside a bucket**: only runes that fit the chosen spell appear, and a bucket with none for that spell shows dimmed. **The rune wheel always opens with its spell already chosen**, so muscle memory forms per spell. Within a bucket runes are alphabetical until custom order exists.
  - **Today's runes:** Trajectory — Salvo, Pierce, Nova, Return, Velocity, Chain, Fork. Area — Expand. Cooldown — Haste. Reaction — Ignition. Utility — Chill, Shroud, Extend (the Ritual's reach, which is why it isn't under Area — that means AoE radius). Duration, Trigger and Tradeoff are empty for now; manual activation and Resonance (Section 9) are planned as Trigger's first.
  - Groupings considered before these: by the chosen spell's own tags plus an any-spell group (compatibility-first, but runes that fit every spell piled into one large group), and by what a rune does with an extra element ring for elemental groups.
- **Greying.** Within a bucket, a rune that conflicts with one already socketed (same type, or a second reaction rune) or is unavailable dims **in place** rather than sorting to the back, since moving runes around as sockets fill works against muscle memory. Whether unlearned runes show dimmed or hidden is open.

## 5. Enemies

**Philosophy: trade-offs, not gates.** Soft pressure (tanky/slow vs. fast/fragile, grouping, timed bursts, partial resistances) over hard immunities. **No elemental resistances at all** — elemental typing is a player-facing build axis only. Difficulty tiers differentiate through composition and timing, never flat stat multipliers.

Roster sketch: Fodder, Ranged Attacker, Tank, Flyer, Sapper, Assassin-type. **Built so far:** `Grunt` (ordinary walker), **Marksman** (ranged), **Sapper** (tower-killer) and **Brute** (heavy melee).

**Enemy variety is subclassing, not composition.** `Enemy` → `WalkingEnemy`/future `FlyingEnemy`/`BossEnemy` → concrete types. An earlier plan called for swappable `IMovementBehavior`/`IAttackBehavior` objects by analogy to `SpellCastContext`'s components, but that composability only pays off when the same behavior is genuinely reused across many combinations, the way `ChainRune` applies identically to any spell. Enemy specialness doesn't work that way — a flying ranged enemy's attack would be its own bespoke kit, not a `RangedAttackBehavior` reused verbatim from a ground archer. Without real interchangeable reuse there's no combinatorial explosion to protect against, so plain subclassing plus Godot's own scene inheritance for content variants is simpler. **Still open:** whether _movement style_ (ground-lane vs. flying) is worth keeping as its own reusable axis — decide when a `FlyingEnemy` actually gets built.

`Enemy` holds only what's universal: health, `TakeDamage`, ailments, elemental-status visuals, registration, a virtual `Die()`. `WalkingEnemy` holds everything lane-following, leash-chasing, wall-engaging and ragdoll-specific. A concrete subclass is only worth creating once an enemy needs code its siblings don't — `grunt.tscn` just attaches `WalkingEnemy.cs` directly.

**Movement.** A `Lane` per spawner computes its route once via a single static navmesh query and exposes it as ordered waypoints with precomputed remaining-distance-to-goal; enemies walk it by index rather than each running a live query every frame. **Walking enemies steer horizontally and fall under gravity**: a baked navmesh sits a little above the real floor (0.3m on the test level), so steering toward a path point's full position would float them at navmesh height — and anything placed at an enemy's feet, like Ice Storm's chilled ground, would float with them. Waypoint arrival is measured horizontally for the same reason. `RoutePreviewEffect`'s build-phase trail reuses the same waypoints.

`Enemy` runs a `MovementMode` state machine — `FollowingLane`, `ChasingPlayer`, `RejoiningLane`, `ApproachingObjective`, `AttackingWall`. Leash and attack checks run on an interval, not every frame.

- **Leash:** an enemy within a small leash range of any player's active Sentinel — the nearest one wins — breaks off its lane advance to chase and attack, giving up and returning to its route if the player gets far enough away. Same "break from default movement, act, resume" shape as wall engagement.
- **Give-up-and-rejoin picks the nearest waypoint across every lane**, not just the one it started on, with one rule: never rejoin behind its own already-covered progress on its own lane. Any point on a lane it never walked is fair game.
- **`NavigationAgent3D` avoidance** is wired with radius pulled from the enemy's own capsule shape rather than duplicated as a separate number. Godot requires `TargetPosition` stay live even during lane-following for avoidance to produce non-zero velocity at all, so this costs one navmesh query per waypoint reached rather than zero — still far cheaper than a live per-frame query per enemy.
- **Known rough edge:** several enemies converging on the same destination still crowd and jostle rather than spreading out. Real level geometry will surface what's worth tuning better than guessing on the test level.
- **Knockback** (`Enemy.ApplyKnockback`, an impulse in kg·m/s) moves a _living_ enemy — separate from the on-death ragdoll launch, since a walking enemy is `CharacterBody3D`-driven rather than free physics. It's divided by `Enemy.Mass` (kilograms, 80 by default — the ragdoll bones carry their own, unrelated masses), so heavy enemies move less but are never immune. **Horizontal only**: a real launch would need a stand-up animation per enemy type. A shove becomes a sliding velocity that bleeds off exponentially (`KnockbackDrag`); while it lasts, the enemy isn't steering, attacking or running engagement checks, and slides straight into walls rather than around them. Only `WalkingEnemy` implements it — the training dummy can't be moved. Repel, Converge's pull and Tank's planned Shield Slam all go through it. Gravity still pulls a shoved enemy down, so one pushed off a ledge falls.
- **After being moved, a lane-walking enemy re-paths** with the ordinary give-up-and-rejoin rather than walking a straight line back to a waypoint that may now be behind a wall. That rejoin waits until the enemy can walk again, so one rooted by Converge doesn't re-path every time the pull nudges it. Every other mode already steers by the nav agent and carries on from wherever it landed.
- **A taunt outranks everything.** `Enemy.ForcedTarget` finds a live `TauntedDebuff` (Retaliation), and `WalkingEnemy` checks it before every other engagement: while it holds, the enemy chases and attacks that Sentinel whatever else it would want, moving 1.5× faster so the taunt window isn't spent walking. Once the taunt lapses — or its taunter dies or leaves play — the ordinary checks take back over, and out of leash range the chase gives up and rejoins the lane like any other. The lookup names the taunt specifically rather than being a hook on every debuff, since nothing else forces targeting; if something ever does, that's when it becomes a shared hook.

**Spawning and group movement need a proper design pass (open).** Today spawning copies Dungeon Defenders: each spawner trickles its enemies out one at a time on `WaveController.SpawnInterval`, and every enemy then walks the same single-line lane independently. That can't express a group — say three enemies walking out abreast and holding that formation down the lane — and there's no notion of a group at all once they're moving. The same independence is behind the bunching: when something slows the front of a column (chilled ground, a wall), everyone behind walks into them, and avoidance fights over the overlap instead of the group reacting together. Worth sitting down on properly rather than patching: how waves describe groups and formations, how a group follows a lane (offset paths, a shared leader, flocking), and how it reacts to slows and obstacles. Expect it to be substantial enemy-AI work, not a spawner tweak.

**Marksman** only ever fires a real traveling `EnemyProjectile`, never hitscan, and opportunistically engages a tower it comes into range of without hunting one down (`RitualsOutrankPlayer = false`). `EnemyProjectile` resolves damage against whatever it actually collides with rather than a target locked in at launch, so it's genuinely blockable by anything in the way. It's also the only thing that sets `DamagePacket.Origin = Projectile`; melee defaults to `Melee` for free.

**Brute** is a ~4m heavy with a club, slow and high-health, and a leash range twice the usual so it's drawn to players. Its attack is a telegraphed swing: a wind-up, then a sweep that hits **every Sentinel and every non-disabled Ritual inside a 150° arc** in front of it when the club passes straight ahead — Rituals as collateral only, since it never seeks them out. A wall or the objective it was attacking is struck directly. Once the swing starts the Brute can neither turn nor walk (`WalkingEnemy.IsCommittedToAttack`) until it recovers, so stepping out of the arc during the wind-up dodges it. The swing is procedural, posing the club arm's bone from code over the walk cycle. **Known rough edge:** the navmesh is baked for normal-sized agents, so the Brute clips corners; a second navmesh baked for large agents is the fix if it matters.

**Sapper** prioritizes towers over both the objective and the player (`RitualsOutrankPlayer = true`), falling back to the objective only once no live tower exists. Ordinary enemies can't damage towers at all — it's an opt-in `WalkingEnemy.CanAttackRituals` flag.

Every enemy attack aims at `IDamageable.AimPoint` rather than a raw `GlobalPosition`, which would be a character's feet or an object's local origin.

**`TrainingDummy : Enemy`** is a dev-only unkillable target for testing Ritual behavior without fighting loot RNG or waiting for a wave. It overrides `Die()` to reset health and never registers a kill, reward or drop, and shows a live rolling DPS readout above its head. Not part of the real roster; lives in the hub (Section 8).

## 6. Items & Progression

**Equipment: 11 slots.** Weapon, 5 Armor (`Helmet`/`Chest`/`Legplates`/`Boots`/`Gloves`), and 5 Accessory (`Belt`, `Amulet`, `LeftRing`/`RightRing`, `Relic`). Accessories are where Ritual-affecting mods live. `Ring` is one class, not two — a dropped ring doesn't know which hand it's for, so the two slots hold the same type.

`Item` is abstract with a real subclass per slot rather than a flat class with an `EquipmentSlot` field. Unlike `SpellCastContext`'s composition case, equipment slots aren't independently-combinable traits: an item is permanently and exclusively one slot, and the set is closed and stable, so this is a genuine is-a hierarchy. `Weapon` is itself abstract with `Gun` and `SwordAndShield` (Tank-only) beneath it — `LootGenerator.CreateItemForSlot` is character-aware so rolling a Weapon for Tank produces a sword. `DefaultItemIcons` is keyed by `System.Type` rather than `EquipmentSlot` because those two share a slot value but need different thumbnails.

**One body per player, swapped rather than hidden.** A player's body is their Sentinel in Planning, Combat and PostGame, and their Architect in Build; at each of those phase changes `SentinelSpawner` replaces it with the other one, standing where the old one stood. The first body appears at the seat's `PlayerSpawnPoint` — every level and the hub has one per seat. The Sentinel is the one the owner's `PlayerProfile.SelectedCharacter` names. Going into Build the outgoing Sentinel revives if dead, refills Health and Shield and stands down, so the HUD shows what the next wave starts with; a fresh Sentinel arrives for Combat with cooldowns reset.

- **What outlives a body lives on `PlayerLevelState`** (`LevelContext.StateOf(PlayerId)`), made with the level and freed with it: the Tank's heat, Harvest's gauge, and the death count that escalates respawn time and cost. The hub has one too, so a hub character swap keeps heat.
- **Stats work without a body.** A Sentinel's base values live on a `SentinelDefinition` resource (`assets/sentinels/`, looked up by `CharacterId` through `SentinelCatalog`) — base health, and on `TankDefinition` what heat does to stats. `SentinelStats` combines definition, gear and level state, and the Sentinel reads its own stats through it too. That's what lets the character sheet show your Sentinel during Build, and the heat gauge stay up (it reads the level state directly).
- `LevelContext.Sentinels` and `ActiveCharacters` are keyed by `PlayerId`. `Sentinel.Local` and `LevelContext.LocalActiveCharacter` are view-side conveniences for this machine's camera and HUD; gameplay code takes a `PlayerId` instead.

**Every player has a `PlayerProfile`** (`PlayerProfiles.Get(PlayerId)`), holding their persistent data — loadouts, tokens, Sentinel pick — and the stat sheets derived from it. The local player's is backed by the save file; another player's is the copy of their save they bring to the match, Dungeon Defenders-style, so the host never needs a second data format. **Gameplay resolves gear through the owning player's profile, never through `SaveManager`**: a Sentinel's stats, a Ritual's equipment bonuses, ability cooldowns and token deployment all go through the owner's `PlayerId`. UI for your own inventory reads the local save directly, since that genuinely is the local player's data. The Sentinel pick (`SelectedCharacter`) writes through to `PlayerSaveData.LastPlayedCharacter`, so it persists across sessions and defaults to whoever you played last.

**Every write to a player's data goes through their profile, and so do the rules for it.** Pickup auto-equip, first-empty-slot placement, the rune-tome cascade, gold spending, tokens and favorite naming are `PlayerProfile` methods, not UI code; UI reads save data freely but never writes it. `CommitChange` is the single save-and-notify path — it persists and fires `InventoryChanged`, which drops every stat sheet and Ritual cache. Gold changes and the crafting slot save immediately, since those are worth protecting from a crash the moment they happen; routine shuffling is debounced. In co-op this is the one place that has to become host-validated.

### Mods

**A shared definition plus a small per-item roll.** `Affix` is the hand-authored definition — name, description template, `AffixType` (Prefix/Suffix/Unique), the `StatId` it affects, allowed slots, character restriction, and a 5-tier value table. **Tier 1 is weakest, 5 strongest — deliberately the reverse of PoE's convention**, so adding a stronger tier later is just a new highest number instead of renumbering everything. These live hardcoded in `AffixTable` keyed by `StatId`, since there are only a few dozen and nothing but hand-edits touches them. `RolledAffix` is the small `Resource` an item actually carries: just the `StatId`, rolled tier and rolled value, looking everything else up through `Affix`. 41 affixes are authored across the Ritual, Sentinel, ability and Weapon pools — 16 of them the per-slot ability mods, built in a loop rather than written out.

`AffixType.Unique` and `Rarity.Unique` are orthogonal — the first describes whether one mod is fixed and non-craftable, the second describes the item as a whole. `Affix.AllowedSlots` restricts where a mod can roll or be crafted, kept on the affix because the crafting table needs to answer "which mods are valid for this item" live. `Affix.RestrictedCharacter` is the character half of the same question and is enforced, though no affix sets it yet — it exists for character-signature weapon mods. Note this is _not_ what produces a "Templar Only" line on a dropped weapon; that's `Item.EquippableCharacter`, a whole-item scope, and both are needed.

**Loot generation.** `LootGenerator` rolls 3–6 affixes (at least 1 prefix, at least 1 suffix, neither exceeding 3) from a flat weighted pool of every eligible (affix, tier) pair at once — mirroring how PoE actually rolls, one weighted draw across every tier of every mod together rather than picking a mod then its tier. Once an affix is drawn, all its tiers leave the pool, along with anything in the same `AffixGroup`: an item gets at most one element-specific Ritual damage mod, while generic Ritual damage sits outside that group and can appear alongside it. Crafting's "add a mod" follows the same exclusion. `Rarity` gates which tiers are reachable (`Common` = T1 only through `Legendary` = all five), each step keeping weaker tiers available; `AffixTierRange.Weight` controls likelihood within that. `Rarity.Unique` is never generated — uniques are meant to be hand-authored with their own signature mod.

Damage resolves as `Base * (1 + IncreasedPercent) * MoreMultiplier`, PoE-shaped, so equipment mods have a clean bucket to plug into.

**Extra damage as an element (built)** adds a share of a hit's base damage as another element before the crit multiplier, so a crit multiplies the added portion too. The Tank's heat is the first source and more are expected, so it's a hook in shared damage building (`Sentinel.AddExtraElementalDamage`, called from `RollAttackDamage`), not Tank code.

**Design rule:** avoid flat "+X% damage" as the _only_ kind of mod. Items should let players break the rules — a unique that collapses a spell's projectiles into one with damage summed — not just tweak numbers.

**Unique idea (not built): Rituals hit much harder while your Sentinel has no weapon equipped** — a build for players who'd rather lean into the tower-defense side than the action side. It only works as a trade-off if going weaponless genuinely costs something, which is why new profiles start with a plain weapon per Sentinel (Section 8) rather than bare-handed: the weaponless state stays a deliberate choice, not where a new player begins.

### How mods reach gameplay

`StatId` is the shared vocabulary mods and consumers both speak. `PlayerProfile.GetSheet(CharacterId)` resolves and caches, per player and character, the summed bonus per `StatId` from equipped items, invalidated on equip/unequip. **The sheet holds bonuses only** — base values stay on whatever field already owned them, and each consumer combines its own base with the sheet's bonus, deciding for itself whether that stat is additive or multiplicative.

Ritual-side stats take an ownership-aware path, since they layer alongside per-instance Rune contributions rather than replacing a field read. `Ritual.OwnerId` is a `PlayerId` — deliberately not a `CharacterId`, so two co-op players who pick the same Sentinel still resolve independently. It's set to whoever placed the Ritual, and resolves against that player's currently selected Sentinel's sheet. Placement previews and costs resolve against the same sheet, so cost-reduction gear lowers what's actually paid. `SpellInstance.Resolve(CharacterSheet ownerSheet = null)` takes the sheet as data rather than reaching for it, keeping `SpellInstance` as ignorant of players and equipment as it already is of what's inside a `Rune`.

`SpellCastContext.ApplyEquipmentBonuses(sheet)` folds simple stats into the same `ScaledStat.Increased` fields Runes use. **Per-element Ritual damage goes into a separate `ElementalIncrease` struct with a private setter specifically so a Rune can never reach that lever** — the same reasoning PoE uses to keep item affixes and support-gem multipliers from cannibalizing each other. Cooldown recovery rate _divides into_ cooldown time exactly as cast speed divides into cast time, matching PoE's real mechanic where recovery-rate stats can't push a value to zero; mana and capacity reduction stay linear and floored at 1. Ritual Area of Effect and Duration only scale a capability the spell (or a rune) already gave it — Area feeds `AreaCastData` (Fireball, Ice Storm), Duration Ice Storm's storm and chilled ground and Ice Wall's cap — so on a spell without one they do nothing, by design. Rituals re-resolve live — `EventBus.InventoryChanged` invalidates every listening Ritual's cached context, so re-gearing mid-Build retroactively updates towers already placed.

**Current Sentinel stat surface, all live end to end:** Health, attack damage, attack speed, move speed, Armor/Shield, Crit Chance/Damage, Health Regen (flat and percent), and the ability stats below. `AttackDamage` and `AttackSpeed` live on `Sentinel` itself, not per-subclass.

### Ability mods

Four kinds, mirroring what Rituals get: **Cooldown Recovery, Area of Effect, Duration and Damage.** Each comes as a **generic** mod (every ability, up to 20% — 32% for damage) and a **per-slot** mod (one slot's abilities, up to 30% — 45% for damage), and the two add together. All armor-only. Damage and Area are prefixes, Cooldown and Duration suffixes. The generic Cooldown one is the pre-existing `SentinelCooldownRecoveryRate`.

- **Per slot, not per ability**, so one item works for every Sentinel: the 16 per-slot stats are `Ability1Area` … `Ability4Damage`, looked up through `AbilityStats` rather than by enum arithmetic. Their text carries an `{ability}` token filled in from the selected Sentinel's slot names (`Affix.ResolvedTemplate`), so the same item reads "+15% Converge / Repel Duration" on a Soldier and names Tank's slot on a Tank. The names exist only on a Sentinel's ability nodes, so each Sentinel records them in `SentinelCatalog` when it spawns, which keeps them available while you're the Architect; before any Sentinel of that kind has existed it falls back to "Ability 1". The Character Sheet uses the same resolved text. A paired slot's two abilities both get its mods.
- **Per-slot mods are weighted at 500 per tier** instead of 1000, so each kind summed across its four slots turns up about twice as often as one ordinary armor mod rather than flooding the pool.
- **What each ability does with them is its own call**, and not every stat helps every ability — deliberately, so a Sentinel indifferent to one stat has room for another. Soldier:

  | Ability          | Area of Effect         | Duration       | Damage                                                                                                                                                                |
  | ---------------- | ---------------------- | -------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
  | Converge / Repel | field radius           | field lifetime | —                                                                                                                                                                     |
  | Visor            | —                      | duration       | the weak-point bonus (1.3× → 1 + 0.3 × (1 + bonus)) — scaling only the bonus part, since scaling the whole multiplier would compound far too hard with headshot crits |
  | Health Link      | spike range            | link duration  | spike damage                                                                                                                                                          |
  | Harvest          | radius                 | —              | —                                                                                                                                                                     |
  | Overdrive        | +1 pierce per full 33% | expiry         | shot damage                                                                                                                                                           |

  Cooldown Recovery applies to every ability. Area multiplies a radius directly, matching how the Expand rune treats area. An ability with no area of its own gets something else from it (Health Link's reach, Overdrive's pierce) rather than nothing.

- **Per-ability effectiveness levers.** Every `Ability` exports `CooldownRecoveryEffectiveness`, `AreaEffectiveness`, `DurationEffectiveness` and `DamageEffectiveness` (default 1), scaling the combined bonus before it's applied — the way to rein in one ability's scaling without touching the mods. The Visor's damage one defaults to 0.5. Values are read when an ability activates, so re-gearing mid-cast doesn't reshape a cast already running; the Visor's multiplier is the exception, read per shot.

### Armor and Shield

**Base values are item-inherent, not rolled affixes.** Each `Armor` piece gets a `BaseType` (`Hybrid`/`Armor`/`Shield`, rolled at generation) and a `RolledBaseStat[]` referencing the shared `ArmorBaseTypeTable` — deliberately not subclassed, since `BaseType` is rolled per instance like an affix rather than fixed per concrete type the way `Gun`/`SwordAndShield` are. Total scales with `Rarity`: Helmet/Gloves/Boots use 100–500 across Common→Legendary, and Chest/Legplates double that via `Armor.SlotWeight`. Hybrid splits 50/50; a pure type puts it all in one stat.

**Shield is worth a quarter as much per point as Armor** (`GameRules.ShieldValuePerArmorPoint`), baked into the stored base value at generation rather than applied at runtime — so a pure-Shield item nets a quarter of what a pure-Armor item does from the same budget, and Hybrid's Shield share drops to an eighth while Armor stays at half. Items rolled before a change to that ratio keep their old values. **Unique-rarity values are still open**, likely hand-set per unique.

**Resolution is local-per-item, a deliberate divergence from every other stat.** `Armor.ResolvedValue(stat)` is that item's own base × that item's own rolled percentage affixes, with no character-level layer. The sheet sums each piece's resolved value into `CharacterSheet.GetLocalTotal(stat)`, a pool kept separate from the `GetBonus(stat)` percentage pool. A global multiplier does exist on top — `SentinelTotalArmor`/`SentinelTotalShield`, jewelry-only affixes applying via the ordinary bonus pool: `Total = GetLocalTotal * (1 + GetBonus(totalStat))`.

**Armor is a percentage reduction; Shield is an absorb-then-regen pool. Armor leans melee, Shield leans projectile.** Every hit goes through both, Armor first — so Armor also slows how fast a hit drains Shield — and whatever Shield doesn't absorb goes to Health.

- **Armor** reduces damage by an S-curve (`Armor^p / (Armor^p + K^p)`) rather than a plain `x/(x+K)`, which grew too fast at low investment; it's tuned so trivial gear barely registers and near-max gear approaches ~90% against melee. Projectiles get half that percentage (`GameRules.ArmorProjectileEffectiveness`) — enough that Armor isn't useless against them, while leaving Shield as the main answer to the projectile damage expected to be most characters' scariest.
- **Shield** absorbs every kind of hit, but each point stops two points of projectile damage (`GameRules.ShieldProjectileEffectiveness`) and one of melee. It passively regens once `GameRules.ShieldRegenDelay` (3.5s) has passed since the last hit of any kind — real time, counted whether or not the shield is full.
- The Character Sheet shows only the melee reduction. Both reset to full on `HealToFull()`. **A gear change clamps current Health and Shield down to the new maximum but never tops them up** — equipping shouldn't double as a heal, and regen closes a raised gap — and the HUD shows the new maximum immediately.

**Overshield** is Shield granted past the maximum (today only by Harvest, Section 3). It lives in the same pool, so a hit drains it first, and tops out at 2× the maximum (`GameRules.OvershieldCeilingMultiplier`). **Any gear change wipes it** — Shield clamps back to the maximum like it always has, which is both the risk of re-gearing mid-fight and what stops unequipping a Shield piece from leaving leftover Shield that reads as overshield. Regen stops at the maximum and never reaches it, and it doesn't decay; `HealToFull()` (Build phase, respawn) drops it. The shield bar fills purple from the top down, as a fraction of the maximum, so at the ceiling the whole bar is purple.

### Inventory, drops and stations

**Inventory** is a fixed 40 slots with player-chosen positions, plus all 11 equipment slots in a PoE-style human-silhouette layout. The panel occupies 40% of screen width, works during Build phase without losing mouse control, and stacks correctly with the Build Menu via `UiPanelStack`.

**Every slot has a one-press use action** (`inventory_use` — right-click, or Square): a Rune Tome is learned, anything else in the grid is equipped straight into its slot, and an equipment slot is unequipped into the grid.

**On a controller**, the inventory opens focused on the top-left grid square and the character stops moving while it's open. Cross picks up and places, Triangle quick-moves (below), and the carried item's icon rides on the focused slot. While carrying, Circle stops meaning "close": a tap puts the item back where it came from (or the first open slot, if that one's since filled), and holding it for 0.5s drops it on the ground — claimed in `_ShortcutInput`, which runs after every `_Input` and before any panel's own close. With a crafting or vendor panel open alongside, L1 and R1 jump focus straight between the two panels. **Not built:** a progress ring for the hold-to-drop.

Two interaction styles work on every slot through a small `SlotHandle` abstraction (`GetItem`/`PlaceItem`/`CanAccept`): click-to-pick-then-click-to-place, and a press-drag-release gesture layered on top without altering it. **A plain click that never drifts past a small pixel threshold falls straight through to the click path, which is what keeps this gamepad-safe** — a controller confirm press never emits the mouse motion the drag path watches for. Placing something displaces whatever was there to the earliest open slot rather than losing it; dropping outside the panel, or into a full inventory, sends the item into the world. Rings are placed explicitly, no auto-assignment.

`ItemDragController` is an autoload holding the shared held-item state and cursor icon, so panels other than Inventory can participate in the same gesture. It also supports **sell targets** (a whole panel rect where releasing counts as selling) and **quick-move targets** (`quick_move`, Ctrl+click, or Triangle on a controller, offers the item to each registered target in turn, with an inventory fallback always tried last). Both `quick_equip` and `quick_move` are named Input Map actions rather than hardcoded modifier checks, so they're genuinely remappable — including to a controller button.

**World drops.** `ItemDropManager` rolls loot the way PoE does: `Enemy.LootRolls` is a count of _independent_ `Enemy.DropChance` rolls per kill, so a kill drops anywhere from 0 to `LootRolls` items. Both are plain per-instance exports, so a bigger enemy just needs a higher number in the Inspector. Slot and rarity are randomized per drop, and a dropped `Weapon` gets a random playable Sentinel as its `EquippableCharacter` rather than always the current one. Each drop spawns a `WorldItem` — a small rarity-colored sphere, jittered so multiple drops don't stack exactly.

**Pickup is nameplate-driven, not raycast-at-a-sphere.** `ItemNameplateController` projects nearby `WorldItem`s into screen space each frame and shows a small rarity-colored nameplate above each; `ItemInteractor.FindTargetAhead()` hit-tests the crosshair against those rects instead of firing a physics ray. Aiming a thin crosshair at a small sphere was genuinely annoying. **Nameplates deliberately never push each other apart** — an earlier distance-sorted version made labels visibly reshuffle as the player approached a cluster, which was worse than the overlap.

Whichever item the crosshair rests on expands into the same rich tooltip Inventory hover uses, **pinned to a fixed screen corner rather than anchored to the item** — anchoring meant the tooltip moved while the player was still trying to hold the crosshair steady to read it. It shifts left only far enough to clear an open panel.

**Crafting table** (hub, Room A) modifies an item's mods after the fact. `CraftingActions` provides the actions plus matching gates:

- **Add / Remove Prefix or Suffix.** Adding draws from `AffixTable` capped by the item's rarity and excludes stats already present; **removal picks uniformly among occupied affixes — the player doesn't choose which one goes**.
- **Reroll Prefix/Suffix 1–3** swaps the mod on that bar for a different random one of the same type, in place. The outgoing mod doesn't block its own replacements, so a same-group sibling can come in; the exact same stat can't.
- **Reroll All** rerolls every mod's value within its own tier range, without touching which mods are present.
- **Upgrade Random Tier** moves a random mod up one tier, rolling a fresh value in the new range. Mods already at the item's rarity cap are excluded, so enough presses always converge on every mod at its best reachable tier.

The per-bar rerolls and tier upgrade make crafting close to deterministic — deliberate for now, as a debugging aid. Each action costs a flat `GameRules.CraftingActionGoldCost` (currently 1), set mostly to give Gold somewhere to go rather than as a tuned price. The panel shows Prefix/Suffix groups of 3 bars each, with empty slots still reading as reserved slots, plus a data-driven action list with live affordability gating. `PlayerSaveData.CraftingSlotItem` persists separately from `Inventory`, since an item on the table isn't in your bag, and saves immediately on every change.

**Vendor** (hub, Room A, mirroring the crafting table) buys, sells and buys back. Stock is 5 items generated fresh via `LootGenerator.GenerateRandomDrop()` each time the hub loads, plus a Buyback list that fills as items are sold. Neither persists — the hub scene fully reloads on every arrival, so this falls out for free. `Item.SaleValue` (mod count; a flat 100 for generic currency) is the one price used in every direction, no markup. Selling is triggered by dragging an item anywhere onto the panel or quick-moving it.

**Stash** (hub, Room A) holds items beyond Inventory: named tabs of 100 slots each (`StashTab`), opening on the left beside Inventory like the vendor and crafting table. Every profile starts with one free tab; each extra costs a flat 10 gold (`GameRules.StashTabGoldCost`). Tabs sit in a sideways-scrolling strip with the buy button at its end, and the current tab's name is edited in place — a blank name reverts to "Tab N". Slots use the same drag, tooltip and quick-move machinery as Inventory; with the stash open, a quick-move from Inventory lands in the current tab's first empty slot and one from the stash goes to Inventory. Tabs live in the profile, so backups and quarantine cover them (a quarantined stash item recovers into Inventory, not the stash). While any text field has focus, keyboard movement is ignored (`UiPanelStack.IsTyping`), so naming a tab doesn't walk the character.

**Not built:** stock-gating by progression, so Legendaries can't appear at the vendor or drop at level 1. Deferred together with the identical gap on enemy drops rather than solved twice.

### Tooltips

A shared `ItemTooltip` autoload replaces Godot's built-in `Control.TooltipText` everywhere — the built-in one lives in an internal popup window that closes itself on Escape, which read as flickering.

It's **event-driven, not polled**: `Register(control, getItem)` wires the control's real hover and focus signals — focus is what a controller moves — subscribes to `ItemDragController.Changed` to catch a slot's contents changing while the mouse never left, and treats the advanced-view modifier as a discrete input event. Only the panel's on-screen position tracks per frame, since that's a genuinely continuous quantity.

- **Hold `advanced_tooltip` (Alt / R3)** for a PoE2-style layout: each mod is an `AffixRowView` row with a right-aligned tier badge, grouped under Prefix/Suffix headers. Rows are single-line — wrapping created a circular width↔height loop with the auto-sizing parent. Hovering a tier badge opens a nested popup with the mod's full name and its `AffixTag[]`.
- **Hold `compare_item` (Shift / L3)** to show the equipped item(s) in the same slot alongside, up to 2 panels — 2 rather than 1 specifically for `Ring`'s dual slots, via `EquippedLoadout.GetEquipped(slot)` yielding both rings when asked for either. It's off Ctrl because Ctrl is the dodge roll, and comparing a ground item mid-level would roll the player.
- **Position anchors to the hovered control, not the mouse** — recomputed while hovered, then frozen once the mouse leaves. This is what lets the mouse catch the tooltip and move into it while a modifier holds it open, instead of the tooltip fleeing the cursor. It flips right when there isn't room on the left, checked against the combined extent of the main panel plus the widest visible comparison panel.
- For `Armor`, resolved Armor/Shield values show under the item name in a distinct blue so they read as a different kind of information.
- **Escape (or Circle) closes every open panel at once**, via `UiPanelStack.CloseAll()` — Godot stops delivering an input event once a handler marks it handled, so only the topmost panel used to see it. `BuildMenu` is the deliberate exception: it steps backward through its own wizard screens one at a time when it's the top panel, and is only swept away in full when something else is above it.

**Explicitly decided against:** a player damage-resistance/type system. It would require giving enemies damage types, which isn't needed unless itemization proves it is.

## 7. Architecture

### Cast pipeline

1. **`Spell`** (e.g. `FireballSpell`) — the immutable definition, shared by every tower using it. No mutable state lives here.
2. **`SpellInstance`** — one per placed tower. A reference to the shared `Spell` plus that tower's attached `Rune` list and cast/cooldown state. This is the tower's loadout, not a projectile.
3. **`SpellInstance.Resolve()`** — applies every attached Rune over the Spell's base values, producing a **`SpellCastContext`**: a transient data snapshot of final stats for one cast.
4. **`CastEffect.Cast(context, spawnPoint, caster)`** — how the spell is delivered (projectile, instant strike, wall, bombardment). The spell picks it in `AddComponents` and it rides on the context as `SpellCastContext.CastEffect`, so a rune could swap delivery like any other field; `Ritual` just calls whatever is there. Effects are plain stateless C# classes shared between Rituals — everything they need, including spell-specific visuals like Lightning's arc scene, arrives on the context. Placement-time choices (a wall's endpoints) arrive on the context itself via the Ritual's `PlacementData`, so no effect needs to reach back into the `Ritual` for them.
5. **`Projectile`** — the flying object, fully independent once spawned, with its own damage, radius, pierce count and hit tracking.
6. **`ProjectileHitEffect`** (e.g. `ExplodeOnHitEffect`) — reacts on hit, and the right extension point for future on-hit behavior _authored on the spell_. On-hit behavior granted by a **rune** goes through `OnHitRune` instead, which reaches every damage path rather than just projectiles — `SpellCastContext.OnHitRunes` travels to `Enemy.TakeDamage` as a `SpellHit`, so a projectile, a hitscan strike, a chain jump and an explosion all run the same runes.

Each arrow hands off to something new and independent; nothing downstream reaches back upstream.

**`SpellCastContext` is one class with optional components, not a subclass tree.** It carries universal fields (`Range`, `Damage` as a `DamagePacket`, costs) plus nullable components — `Projectile`, `Chain`, `Area`, `InstantHit`, `Wall`, `CastTrigger` — each null unless needed. A Spell's `AddComponents()` decides which it starts with, and a Rune can lazily create one it didn't (`ChainRune` grants chaining to a spell with no `Chain` component at all).

The original subclass design broke as soon as a spell needed two capabilities with no is-a relationship between them: `FireballSpell` is both Projectile and AoE, neither specializes the other, and C# allows one inheritance path. Patching with `AoeProjectileCastContext`-style classes means a new class per _combination_, and silently assumes every spell sharing a base wants every capability it carries — untrue for a single-target Ice Spear with no use for `Area`. Independently-combinable capabilities live as nullable components checked with `!= null`, borrowing the ECS idea narrowly for this one class rather than adopting it project-wide.

**Where a stat's base value lives:** universal stats (Range, Cooldown, Damage, RagdollForce, costs, target priority) are flat fields on the context, filled in once by the base `Spell.CreateBaseContext()` so no spell can forget one; capability-specific ones (ProjectileCount, ChainCount, AreaMultiplier) live on the relevant component, which a spell's `AddComponents()` only creates if the spell has that capability.

**Swappable hooks over raw fields.** Three cases follow the same shape — an optional `Resource` hook rather than a field on shared data:

- **`ExplosionRadius`** stays on `ExplodeOnHitEffect`, not threaded through `ProjectileCastData`. Those types describe what `Projectile` itself acts on; explosion behavior belongs entirely to the swappable hit-effect strategy, which exists so `Projectile` never needs to know about it. `fireball_spell.tres` embeds its own effect as a local sub-resource, so the radius is authored right there and can't be stomped by another spell.
- **`ChainDamageModifier`** (`GetMultiplier(remainingChains)`) replaced a raw `MoreDamagePerRemainingChain` float. `ChainCastData` is shared by every chaining spell, so a raw field there implied any spell could use it when it only ever described Lightning Bolt's finisher curve.
- **`CastTrigger`** gates whether a cast happens at all. The base is generic so a future trigger-rune can supply its own; the one that exists today, `WallProximityCastTrigger`, is Ice Wall's.

**`Projectile.Radius`** (physical size, unrelated to blast radius) _is_ threaded through the context, since scaling its own mesh and collision is core `Projectile` behavior. That scaling is uniform on all axes and assumes a roughly round mesh — a future elongated projectile would need its own formula for that one line.

**`ChainableSpell` is reserved for spells where chaining is inherent to identity** (`LightningBoltSpell`), not for projectiles that merely _can_ chain if a `ChainRune` is socketed. `FireballSpell` extends `Spell` directly and declares nothing about chaining. `ChainRune` carries its own `FallbackChainRange` applied via `Mathf.Max`, so a rune-granted chain always has reach without ever shrinking a spell's inherent range.

### Class notes

- **Rune** — tier, required/granted tags, the data transformation it applies, any event hooks it registers. A `Rune` is a shared `Resource`: the same `.tres` is referenced by every Ritual socketing it, so a rune can never cache per-Ritual state on itself. Anything resolved per-cast belongs on the `SpellCastContext`, and anything needed at hit time has to arrive through the `OnHitContext`.
- **Ability** — one of a Sentinel's four active slots, and deliberately a **`Node`, not a `Resource`** — the one place the "gameplay values live on a Resource" rule doesn't apply. A Resource can't own scene content, can't call `CreateTween()`, and has no update loop, and abilities need all three (a tether visual between two enemies, a gauge, per-enemy markers). Being a Node also collapses the definition/instance split `Spell`/`SpellInstance` needs: a Node is inherently per-instance, so cooldown and duration state live on it with no shared asset to stomp, and `_PhysicsProcess` is the tick for free. The precedent is the `RitualPlacer`/`TokenDeployer`/`LookInteractor` components a Sentinel already carries. The base stays thin — `DisplayName`, `Description`, `BaseCooldown`, `CooldownFraction`, `IsActive`, `CanToggleOff`, `TryActivate()` — with behavior bespoke per subclass. Numbers use the `[Export] public override float BaseCooldown { get; protected set; } = 20f;` pattern `FireballSpell` uses, so the C# initializer is the default and the Inspector is an optional override serialized only if touched.
  - **Cooldown starts when the ability ends, not when it begins**, so a duration-based ability cancelled early recovers from where it actually stopped. `SentinelCooldownRecoveryRate` divides into `BaseCooldown` there, matching `SpellCastContext.ResolvedCooldown`.
  - **The four slots are an explicit exported array, not auto-discovered from child order** — slot order _is_ the key binding, so auto-discovery would silently remap keys if nodes were ever reordered.
  - **Tap/hold is a slot feature, not an ability one.** `Sentinel.HoldAbilitySlots` runs parallel to `AbilitySlots`, so a slot can hold a second, separate ability fired on a hold. A slot with one defers every press: releasing before `GameRules.AbilityHoldThreshold` (0.3s) fires the tap ability, reaching it fires the hold ability at that moment; a slot without one fires on press with no delay. Losing ability input mid-hold (death, token pick mode) drops the press rather than firing it later. A future setting swaps which is tap and which is hold.
  - **A slot's two abilities never run at once** — neither activates while its slot partner is active. That one rule gives Converge / Repel "one field at a time" and Harvest / Overdrive "no harvesting mid-Overdrive" without either ability knowing about the other. A shared cooldown is separate and opt-in: `Ability.CooldownPartner`, wired both ways in the scene, puts the partner on cooldown whenever this one starts one. Harvest and Overdrive deliberately aren't partners, since Overdrive has no cooldown and Harvest's shouldn't lock it out.
  - **`CanActivate()`** is the last check before activating, for an ability with its own precondition — somewhere to place a field, a full gauge to spend. Failing it means no cast and no cooldown.
  - **`LocksOutOtherAbilities`** — while an ability with it is active, `Sentinel` won't start any other; one already running carries on, and the locking ability can still be pressed again (Retaliation's stance).
  - **Handing back to the Architect ends what's running.** When Build starts, `Sentinel` cancels any capture and calls `OnCharacterDeactivated()` on every ability. Vent, Weak-Point Visor and Overdrive end (unfired Overdrive shots are lost), an Immolate channel cancels for free; Retaliation ends quietly — no blast, no heat — and goes on cooldown. Anything left out in the world is the ability's own call: Converge/Repel fields and dud Health Link spikes are left to run out.
  - **Sentinel-side hooks abilities lean on:** `CanMove` (on `PlayerCharacter` — plants the feet while still allowing look and actions; dodge refuses too), `IncomingDamageMultiplier` (scales every hit before armor or Shield sees it), `SentinelDefinition.ArmorMultiplier` (a swing on top of gear armor, on the definition so it works without a body), `AddExtraElementalDamage` (an elemental share of each attack roll, before crits) and `OnDied` (subclass state death should clear). The Tank's heat and Retaliation are the current users.
  - **An ability that targets with the mouse takes the attack binding, through three separate gates.** `CanUseAbilityInput` (activation, and an ability reading its own clicks), `CanUseWeaponInput` (the character's gun or sword), and `ExtraCombatInputGate` underneath both. The capture term lives only in the weapon gate: folding it into `ExtraCombatInputGate` is circular, because `CanUseAbilityInput` is built on top of that, so a capturing ability would gate away the very clicks it's trying to read. The weapon is suppressed by the gate rather than by `SetInputAsHandled()`, since `Soldier`/`Tank` poll `Input.IsActionPressed` in `_PhysicsProcess` and never see the event.
  - **A click an ability took never becomes weapon fire**, even held past the moment its capture ends. Because the weapon polls, a held click that closed a capture (Health Link's second spike) would otherwise fire the rifle on the very next frame. So the weapon only fires off a press that reached the `Sentinel` unhandled — abilities are its children, so they see the event first and a press they consume never gets there.
  - **Escape backs out of a capture** unless the ability sets `CanCancelCapture` false (Overdrive), in which case Escape falls through to opening the pause menu instead of being swallowed. `Sentinel.IsAnotherAbilityCapturing` lets a long-running capture stand aside for a short one layered on top of it.
  - **Gauges.** An ability can expose `GaugeFraction` and `GaugeElement`; `AbilityGauge` shows one and hides for a Sentinel with none. An active ability's gauge wins over an idle one's, which is how Overdrive's draining shots replace Harvest's charge on the pill while it runs. Tank's heat is expected to reuse it, plus an equilibrium mark.
  - **The ability bar** shows a paired slot under both names ("Converge / Repel") and one sweep: whichever of the two is active, otherwise the longer of their cooldowns.
- **Ritual** — HAS-A `SpellInstance`. The Ritual owns position, capacity cost and HP/disabled state; the instance owns attached runes. Keeps "runes return to inventory on destruction" clean and lets one Spell definition be shared safely across many towers.
- **EffectVolume** — an `Area3D` that affects whatever is inside it, on an interval, for a duration, then frees itself. Subclasses decide what "affects" means; it owns only occupancy, timing and cleanup. **An Area3D rather than a per-tick spatial scan**: the broadphase reports entry and exit instead of sampling, so an enemy clipping the edge between ticks can't be missed, and a large enemy brushing the boundary counts by its actual shape rather than by its origin. Named for what it _does_ rather than for what its subclasses are — a storm and a patch of frozen ground are not the same kind of thing, and the shared base asserts only that both are volumes that tick.
  - **`GroundEffect`** is the terrain family (`ChilledGround`, later a burning one, maybe a wet one). It knows nothing about what spawned it, so a cast effect, a rune firing off a destruction signal, or a reaction can each drop one in a line. Its strength (`Magnitude` — the slow fraction, for chilled ground) is handed in by whatever spawns it, so the number lives on that spell or rune rather than on the terrain's scene. It applies its effect on entry as well as on tick, and the debuff it applies outlives one tick — which is why nothing has to track departures, and why there's no debuff-removal API.
  - **`Bombardment`** is a cast in progress, not terrain, and deliberately _not_ a `GroundEffect`. It sits beside that family rather than inside it.
  - **Sizing happens in code, never by scaling the root.** The collision shape is rebuilt fresh per instance from the resolved radius — a shape authored in a `.tscn` is shared by every instance of that scene — and only the footprint mesh scales, since scaling the root would stretch every child visual with it.
- **Hit** — everything about one hit, handed whole to `IDamageable.TakeDamage`: the `DamagePacket`, its `Attribution` (the `PlayerId` whose Ritual or Sentinel dealt it, plus the source node; no player for an enemy's attack), a `HitType`, the ragdoll push, and the spell's on-hit runes when it came from a spell. `HitType` replaces what used to be flag arguments: `Direct` sets Status and reaches debuffs, `Unmarked` (an Overdrive shot) reaches debuffs but deliberately sets no Status, `DamageOverTime` (a Burn tick) reaches debuffs but sets no Status, `Mirrored` (a Health Link copy) does neither. Attribution follows the damage through Burns and mirrors, so a Burn your tower lit is still yours. Nothing consumes it yet — kill credit and per-player damage stats are the expected first readers — and a new piece of hit information is a field on `Hit`, not another parameter through every damage site.
- **Debuff** — temporary state in a list on an Enemy rather than special-cased into the Enemy class. Covers slows, roots, CC, links between two enemies, elemental effects, and flags such as "currently carrying the income spirit." Three hooks: `Tick` for time-based effects, `OnHostDamaged` for anything reacting to its host being hit, and `SpeedMultiplier` for anything modifying movement (folded together multiplicatively by `Enemy.DebuffSpeedMultiplier`, which `WalkingEnemy.Speed` applies over its `BaseSpeed`). A debuff's numbers are resolved and baked in when it's applied (`BurnAilment` takes a total, not a reference to whatever lit it) — nothing needs to re-resolve against a caster's live stats later. One that deals damage does keep the `Attribution` of the hit that started it, purely so the credit follows the damage; it never reads anything from it.
  - **An ailment is a debuff with an element** — `Debuff.Elements` non-empty, `IsAilment` true: Burn, Immolation, Chill, Chilled Ground. A property rather than a subclass, because a debuff can be a general mechanic and elemental at once — Chill is a `SlowDebuff` that happens to be Cold, and C# can't inherit from both a slow base and an ailment base. The distinction is so elemental things (an elementalist Sentinel, Immolate, a Harvest talent) can act on ailments and ignore plain debuffs like Converged, Health Link or a taunt. `Elements` is a list so a hybrid ailment can credit each of its elements evenly. Files follow it too: `combat/debuffs/`, with the elemental ones under `ailments/`.
  - **Stacking is one general rule, not a policy per debuff.** `OccupiesSameSlotAs` answers "is there already one of me here" — one per type by default, and Health Link widens it to one per _partner_, since links to two different enemies must coexist. When two land in the same slot, `ResolveAgainst` decides, defaulting to strongest-wins on `Magnitude`: an equal application refreshes the duration, a weaker one is discarded outright, so re-casting a weak effect can never keep a strong one alive. `BurnAilment` is the one exception, overriding that to merge remaining damage instead of competing. A debuff meant to stack freely returns false from `OccupiesSameSlotAs` for everything and simply accumulates instances — **a cap on stacks isn't supported**, because the slot check only ever sees one other debuff and can't count the list.
  - **Planned with hard CC, not built: `IsRecovering`.** Today being in the list and counting as present are the same thing. A hard-CC ailment with a cooldown (Section 9, Crowd-control limits) is the first debuff where they differ: after its active duration, Frozen stays in its slot to block reapplication while doing nothing at all. `Debuff.IsRecovering` (default false) marks that window — still holding its slot, no effect, and not counted as present:
    - **`Enemy.DebuffTypes` skips recovering debuffs**, so `HasDebuff` on the host, the synced number and every visual built on it all treat a recovering Frozen as absent. Other players' machines stop drawing ice the moment the freeze ends, not when the cooldown does.
    - **`IsAilment` is false while recovering**, so anything that acts on ailments — a Harvest talent stripping them, Detonate popping them — doesn't see it. Otherwise stripping a recovering Frozen would get something for nothing and delete the cooldown with it.
    - **`Enemy.DebuffSpeedMultiplier` skips recovering debuffs.**
    - **What doesn't change:** `Type` stays the debuff's fixed identity and network tag, the slot rule still compares by class (so the recovering Frozen still blocks new ones), and it still ticks, which is what runs its cooldown down and removes it.
    - **Ask through `HasDebuff`, not a class check.** `HasDebuff(Frozen)` respects `IsRecovering`; walking the list for `is FrozenAilment` finds the recovering one and gets the wrong answer. Today's class checks (Immolation finding a Burn, `ForcedTarget` finding a taunt) are on debuffs with no recovering window, so they're unaffected.
    - One flag rather than separate "what I currently present" overrides for the type flag and elements, since a debuff only ever has these two states.
  - **Considered and shelved: slot keys, and splitting `DebuffType` by job.** Recorded so it isn't re-derived. The idea: each debuff exposes a slot key (its class, plus a parameter such as Health Link's partner) instead of implementing `OccupiesSameSlotAs`, and `DebuffType`'s three jobs — local queries, the tag a debuff carries when a copy sends it to the host, and the synced summary — become a plain wire ID plus published summary flags. It was motivated by per-reaction cooldowns, which would have needed several parameterized debuffs side by side. Keying the cooldown by ailment instead fits the existing one-per-class rule, and Health Link's comparison override works fine as the only parameterized debuff, so nothing needs it.
    - **Storage stays a list regardless.** A dictionary keyed by slot was rejected: the list is changed mid-iteration on purpose (a DoT tick deals damage, which notifies every debuff, whose hooks can add or remove debuffs or kill the enemy), which a backwards index walk tolerates and a C# `Dictionary` throws on; list order is apply order and decides tick order, and with it which damage-over-time tick lands a kill and gets credit; and at a handful of debuffs per enemy a hash lookup buys nothing.
    - **The bit budget is real but cheap.** 7 of 32 bits are used, and the planned ailments and CC could approach 32 eventually. The answer is widening the enum to 64 bits (`long`) when it gets close, not a redesign. `SyncedDebuffTypes` keeps its name either way, since five enemy scenes' synchronizers reference it by path.
    - **Tenacity will need "is this enemy under any hard CC?"** That can be as small as a virtual `IsHardCC` on the debuff, added when tenacity is built.
- **LevelContext** — everything that belongs to one loaded scene: the live-instance registries (`Enemies`, `Rituals`, `IceWallSegments`, `WorldItems`, `FloatingCombatTexts`), the per-player body lookups, spawn points and `PlayerLevelState`s, and scene-wide effects like the Weak-Point Visor. `LevelRoot` is where anything the level creates at runtime goes — projectiles, placed Rituals, dropped items, effects — so it's freed with the level rather than outliving it under the persistent main scene. A node inside the scene rather than static state, so it's freed with the scene and a scene change needs no manual cleanup. Registrants keep the instance they registered with and unregister from that same one, so an outgoing scene's teardown can never touch the incoming scene's lists. **Every level scene needs one as the first child of its root** — without it the first registrant throws.
- **DefendableObjective** — generic name for the shell/harvester loss-condition object so flavor can change without renaming code. It's a passive `IDamageable`; enemies attack it through the same melee loop used on the player, gated by an `ApproachRadius` that accounts for its physical bulk.
- **Wave authoring is JSON, not Resources.** A level's waves live in one file (`assets/waves/test_level.json`, named by the level's `WaveController.WaveFilePath`) — an ordered array of waves, each an ordered array of steps, where a step is either a `spawn` burst of `{lane, enemy, count}` triples or an explicit `wait`. A wave reads top to bottom the way you'd narrate it: "5 of this, wait 10, then 5 of this and 2 of that." Bursts still trickle per-lane on `WaveController.SpawnInterval` so they don't stack-spawn instantly. Deserialized via `System.Text.Json` into plain POCOs, with `EnemyCatalog` resolving string enemy names to `PackedScene` and throwing loudly on a typo. **Accepted trade-off:** enemy references are string-keyed, so a bad name fails at load rather than at edit time. The Resource-array version it replaced needed a fresh sub-resource per spawn entry with no reuse and had a free-text unvalidated spawner id.

### Folder layout

**Organized by domain, not by file type.** A `scripts/` tree used to hold nearly half the project split into `data/` and `systems/`, which is the type-based split [Godot's own guide](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/best_practices/project_organization.html) advises against — and the seam leaked, with `CharacterSheet` in `systems/` while `RarityColors` sat in `data/`. It's gone, redistributed into `spells/`, `items/`, `combat/`, `waves/`, `save/`, `core/` and `debug/` alongside the existing feature folders, with `levels/` holding scenes and their scripts together.

**The project declares no C# namespaces at all**, so moving a file is pure relocation — no code changes, only `res://` path rewrites in `.tscn`/`.tres`/`project.godot`.

**A level is its own content plus two shared scenes.** Each level scene holds only what genuinely varies per level: a `LevelContext` (first child), geometry, lights and environment, the objective, the lanes and their `EnemySpawner`s, a `WaveController` pointed at the level's wave file, and four `PlayerSpawnPoint`s, one per seat. Everything else comes from two instanced scenes: `levels/level_systems.tscn` (economy, loot, game-over handling, debug keys, route preview, a `SentinelSpawner` per seat — levels only) and `ui/player_hud.tscn` (crosshair, health/shield, ability and token bars, respawn overlay, phase bar, nameplates, inventory, character sheet, pause and options menus — the hub uses it too, where the phase bar hides itself). Shared-scene nodes never hold references into the level that instances them; they look level content up through `LevelContext` (e.g. `PrimaryObjective` for the loot spray), which is what keeps them reusable and clear of the inherited-scene export hazard below. `levels/test_level/` is the one level so far, and a test bed rather than shipping content.

**Lighting is shared too.** The hub and every level point their `WorldEnvironment` at one `levels/default_environment.tres` — procedural sky, sky-lit ambient and reflections, AgX tonemapping, light SSAO and glow — so tuning it tunes everything. The sun (`DirectionalLight3D`, shadows on) stays per level, since its angle is a per-map choice, and a level wanting its own mood points at a different resource. Clouds aren't built; Godot's sky materials have none, so they'd be a small custom sky shader.

Content (spells, runes, enemies, items) is data-driven via `Resource` assets so iteration doesn't slow as the roster grows.

### A real hazard

**Don't use a typed `[Export]` to reference a node from an inherited base scene.** `Sentinel` used to reach `RitualTokenPlacer`/`TokenDeployer` that way, and adding one unrelated export was enough for Godot to silently re-serialize `soldier.tscn` with both assignments dropped — the cast fails during scene-build, the property reads null, and since only non-default values are written, the next save omits it. **It fails silently.** The tell: exports pointing at built-in types (`SpringArm3D`, `Camera3D`) survived the same save, because those resolve natively without the C# assembly loaded; only the custom-typed ones were lost. Resolve these with `GetNode<T>()` in `_Ready()`, which is what `LookInteractor` already does for the mirror case. Only `soldier.tscn`/`tank.tscn` use scene inheritance at all — every other `node_paths` entry points at a node local to its own scene, which is safe.

### Save format

Profiles are JSON written by our own code (`SaveCodec`, `SaveFile`), not Godot's `ResourceSaver`. The `.tres` format it replaced stored each class by its script path, each asset by its file path and each enum as a number, so renaming `Legs.cs` to `Legplates.cs` broke a save outright — and inserting a value mid-`StatId` would have silently turned existing affixes into different stats. It also let a shared save run an embedded script on load. Everything below is keyed to what survives a rename instead:

- **Classes by `[SaveTag]`** — a permanent short name on each saved class (`"chest"`), mapped in `SaveTypes`. **Never change a tag once saves exist**; a renamed class keeps its old one. A new saved class needs one, and startup throws if two share a tag.
- **Project assets by Godot UID**, path as a fallback. A tagged class is written out in full; any other `Resource` (a `Spell`, a `Rune`, an icon) is a project asset, written as a reference.
- **Enums by name**, so reordering an enum is safe.
- **What's saved is every `[Export]` property**, walked by C# reflection. Renaming one, or an enum value, needs a migration.
- **Schema version plus migrations (`SaveMigrations`)**: an ordered list of steps, each upgrading the whole file one version. Versions are labels starting at "0.1"; the list is cleared at 1.0, when pre-release saves are thrown away. A file from a newer build is left untouched.
- **Nothing is silently dropped.** Whatever can't be decoded — an item with an unknown tag, a missing asset, an unknown enum name, a field that no longer exists — goes to a **quarantine** list in the file with the reason, logged as a warning on load. An item is set aside on its own wherever it sits; anything else, as one field, list element or dictionary entry of the profile. Every load puts quarantined pieces back (items into the first free Inventory slot) before migrations and decoding run, so a fix shipped later recovers them with no code of its own. Probably unneeded once the format settles; useful while items are being hand-made and classes still move around.
- Profiles from before the format were converted once at startup, kept as JSON only if the new file read back identically; the `.tres` is kept beside it as `.tres.converted`.
- **Items between machines** use the same code (`SaveCodec.EncodeItem`/`DecodeItem`), so only tagged classes can be built from another player's data.

### Multiplayer

Built on Godot's high-level multiplayer API: RPCs, `MultiplayerSpawner` and `MultiplayerSynchronizer`.

- **Trust split: each player owns their own character, the host owns the world.** A client decides where its own Sentinel is and whether its own shots and swings land; the host decides everything shared — enemies, economy, Rituals, phases, win/loss. Waiting on the host for your own movement and aim would feel laggy, and invite-only co-op makes cheating a non-concern. Every change to shared state is a request the host checks and then broadcasts.
- **A player hosts (listen server); no dedicated servers.** Up to 4 players, one per `PlayerId` seat. The host shows as P1, and joiners take the next free seat.
- **The hub is the lobby — there's no single-player/multiplayer choice.** Offline, the local machine is already peer 1, which is exactly what the host is, so single-player is just hosting with nobody connected and needs no conversion to start hosting. Joining is the real transition: the joiner's hub reloads as a copy of the host's. Joining is hub-only; a match in progress can't be joined.
- **"Multiplayer" means another player is connected, not that a session exists.** An open lobby with nobody in it still pauses like single-player. A player joining while the host is paused unpauses the game.
- **Transport is the one swappable piece.** Everything above talks to Godot's high-level API; only the session code knows the peer type. **Player-facing multiplayer is Steam-only** — there is no LAN or join-by-IP for players. ENet survives purely as a developer path, started by launch flags (`--host`, `--join <ip>`), because Steam can't connect a machine to itself and Run Instances on one PC is the fastest way to test co-op. Epic or console multiplayer would each be their own transport work, to be faced if and when.
- **Steam (not built).** The chosen integration is the GodotSteam GDExtension and its `SteamMultiplayerPeer`, driven from C# through a thin wrapper beside `NetworkSession`, since GodotSteam's own C# bindings lag its releases. Steam's relay handles NAT and network isolation, so no port forwarding. **There is no Join button:** joining arrives from Steam — an accepted invite, a friend's "Join Game", or the game launched from an invite — and the game just follows the lobby it's handed. The hub stays the lobby; a **Party** section in the pause menu lists who's here and offers Invite, Leave, the host's Kick, and a privacy setting — **Friends** (the hub is a joinable friends-only lobby), **Invite only**, or **Offline** (no lobby at all), **defaulting to Offline** so co-op is opt-in. The game must stay fully playable without Steam's online services — Steam offline mode just hides Party and Invite — so singleplayer never depends on a lobby existing.
- **Host migration (planned, not built).** Today losing the host drops everyone back to their own hub. The plan has two tiers. First, alongside Steam: the lobby passes to another member, who starts hosting, and everyone reconnects into the new host's hub — the match is lost but the party stays together. Then, resuming at wave boundaries: during Planning and Build the world is small enough for any client to rebuild — every client already holds each Ritual's spell, runes and placement, the economy and the objective's health — so a host lost then resumes where it left off, and one lost mid-Combat restarts that wave from Build. Resuming mid-wave is out of reach: an enemy's real state (debuffs, AI, pathing) and the wave's progress exist only on the host.
  - **Seats outlive the host**, since Ritual ownership, attribution and `PlayerLevelState` are keyed by seat. A seat is tied to the player's Steam identity rather than handed out by join order, and the P1–P4 label players see is derived for display — host first, then join order — so the host still shows as P1 after a migration without anything re-keying. Anything per-player that mustn't change mid-match (a future player color) keys off the seat, not the label; spawn points go by display order at level load. With Steam in, a player's Steam name replaces the P-number in most places anyway.
- **Levels load under a persistent main scene.** `levels/main.tscn` is the project's main scene and never unloads; `LevelLoader` holds the current level (the hub counts as one) under its `Level` node and switches it. Only the host loads a level — through a `MultiplayerSpawner`, so every client gets the same one, including anyone joining later — which is the pattern Godot's own scene-replication guidance recommends over `change_scene_to_file()` in a session. Clients get **Leave Session** in the pause menu in place of Return to Hub. A switch is a short handshake: every machine stops sending sync updates for the outgoing level before the host removes it, so nothing arrives for a node that's already gone. Leaving, or losing the host, drops the level before closing the connection and going solo — otherwise, for a frame, every copy would find itself in charge.
- **One body per player.** The owning player asks their seat's `SentinelSpawner` for a body and the host spawns it everywhere, with the owner as its authority; every other machine's copy is a puppet synced by `MultiplayerSynchronizer`. The host swaps each player to their Architect for Build and back to their Sentinel for Combat. Anything that has to outlive a body — heat, Harvest gauge, deaths — lives on the player's `PlayerLevelState`. When a player leaves mid-match their body goes, the match carries on, and their Rituals stay.
- **The match runs on the host.** `WaveController` runs phases, waves, the ready check (every filled seat must ready) and win/loss on the host and broadcasts each change; a machine whose level loads late asks for the current state.
- **Enemies are simulated only by the host.** `EnemyRoster` spawns each one on every machine under a unique name (`Grunt_12`); copies are kept in step by a synchronizer — movement, health, Status, and which types of debuff they carry (`DebuffType`, one bit each, so a copy can answer "is it burning?") — and animate their walk from how far they moved. Training dummies are host-owned like any enemy, so a dummy's DPS readout counts every player's damage. The objective's health is the host's too, shown on every screen.
- **Copies move smoothly through snapshot interpolation.** Every copied body — enemies, other players' Sentinels and Architects — has a `Motion` node (`MotionPlayback`) that shows it slightly in the past, blended between the two updates either side of that moment. Updates are stamped with the sender's clock, and the delay adapts: two update intervals plus measured arrival jitter, 30–150 ms, so a smooth LAN plays back tighter than a jittery connection. Hits use what you see: a player's own machine decides whether their shots land, against the copy on their screen.
- **Damage goes to whoever owns the target.** A hit on an enemy copy is sent to the host, which credits it to the sender's seat and resolves Status, debuffs and death; knockback travels the same way. An enemy's hit on a player's Sentinel is sent to that player's machine, where dodge, block, armor, Shield, heat and Retaliation apply; going down and standing back up show on every screen. Marksman shots fly as harmless copies on the other machines.
- **Each player sees only the damage numbers credited to them.** Your own direct hits show instantly; the host sends the rest — burn ticks, Health Link mirrors, your Rituals' hits.
- **Your own hits drop an enemy's health bar instantly.** A copy's bar shows the lower of the host's health and a local guess that includes your unconfirmed hits, so a sync that hasn't caught up can't bounce it back. The guess lapses after half a second — covering a hit the host rejected — and is dropped whenever the host's health rises. Death still waits for the host. The guess is exact as long as damage isn't modified on the enemy's side; a future resistance or damage-taken debuff must be something a copy can compute, or its magnitude must sync.
- **The economy is the host's.** `EconomyManager` changes only on the host and every other machine mirrors it; anything a joiner spends — skipping a respawn, placing, repairing — is a request. Every kill feeds the shared pool, and at victory every player's save gets the victory bonus plus all the gold earned in the match.
- **Rituals are placed by asking the host.** The placing machine shows the ghost and runs the usual checks, then `RitualRoster` asks the host, which prices the Ritual against its owner's gear, pays for it and spawns it everywhere. Spells and runes travel as resource paths; an Ice Wall's endpoints travel with the request. A token is spent only once its Ritual is actually placed. Only the host runs Rituals — everywhere else they're copies whose health the host keeps in step — and any player can repair or destroy any Ritual.
- **Gear is shared as stat totals.** Rituals scale with their owner's gear, so `ProfileSync` sends each player's resolved stat totals and Sentinel pick to every machine whenever they change and whenever someone joins. The items themselves never leave their owner's save.
- **Other players' placements are visible.** While someone places, everyone else sees a white ghost with their seat above it — the Ritual and its range, and an Ice Wall as it's dragged out.
- **Ritual casts are replayed, not re-run.** `SpellEffects` carries everything a Ritual's cast puts in the world to the other machines: arcs, explosions, projectiles (launched, turned on each chain or return, and ended by the host), Bombardment and ground effects, and Ice Walls, which each machine's copy of the Ritual raises from its own placement data. The host decides every target and hit, so a chain jumps to the same enemies on every screen, and the copies never deal damage.
- **Abilities work the same from any machine.** Anything that changes an enemy goes through the enemy's own methods — damage, knockback, debuffs, taking its Status, linking — and a copy forwards each to the host, so an ability needs no network code of its own. A debuff travels as its type plus its numbers (`Debuff.ToNetwork`); a taunt's target is the sender's own Sentinel on the host. Health Link sends the pair as one request, so both halves land or neither does.
- **Other players' Sentinels look like what they're doing.** Continuous body state rides the body's synchronizer — the dodge roll, the Tank's sword and shield, the shield's block colors, the weapon being put away, the blade's heat glow (so another Tank's heat shows), the Soldier's Overdrive tint and the rifle's aim. One-shot effects (tracers, impacts, Shield Slam and Retaliation waves, Immolate explosions) are RPCs that run on the owner and everyone else at once. Lasting ones (Vent's aura, Immolate's ring, Retaliation's circle, Converge and Repel fields, Health Link's bolts, spikes and beam) are started and ended by the owner; the rings and circles run their own clocks, since a puppet body doesn't process.
- **Each machine rolls its own player's loot.** The host announces every kill's drop chance and roll count, and each machine rolls, holds and sprays only its own player's drops — so instanced loot needs no owner tags, and loot never has to cross the network. At victory the objective vanishes on every screen, loot or not.
- **Trading is dropping.** An item dropped from an inventory is shared: the host spawns it on every machine through `WorldItemRoster`, and a pickup asks the host, which hands it to exactly one player — whoever asked first — and only if they have room. Items cross the network in the save format (Section 7, Save format), so a new item type or field travels with nothing added, and only tagged classes can be built from another machine's data.
- **Not yet:** a Ritual's aim turns only on the host (it's the invisible spawn point today — worth syncing once a Ritual model has a turning part), and the Steam transport. Two instances on one PC need different `--profile` numbers (Section 8), or they share a save.

## 8. Hub & Dev Tools

A deliberately bare-bones staging area — meshes and colored boxes, no art pass — of two rooms connected by a hallway (`res://levels/hub/hub.tscn`, the first level `LevelLoader` loads).

- **Room A — selection and stations.** `CharacterSelectStation` lists Soldier/Tank and swaps the player's live body on pick. `LevelSelectStation` lists playable levels and loads one through `LevelLoader`; only the host can use it. The crafting table and vendor (Section 6) sit in the remaining corners, and the **stash** chest against the west wall between the vendor and level select. All stations share one `StationInteractor`: a station just implements `IInteractable` (`GetPromptText`/`Interact`), so a new station type never needs new interactor wiring.
- **Room B — Architect DPS testing.** `ArchitectSelectStation` toggles the player into the Architect to place Rituals and test builds without a real match. Pressing it again, or walking out of the room (`ArchitectTestZone`), swaps back to the selected Sentinel. Training dummies are placed in groups of **1 / 4 / 8** across three corners, to separate single-target from multi-target and AoE testing.
- **Unlimited economy, hub-only.** `EconomyManager.Unlimited` short-circuits every mana and capacity check. `WallPlacementFlow`'s segment-affordability check reads the same flag directly, since otherwise an Ice Wall's _drag length_ stayed capped even though placing it was free. Chosen over a huge starting pool so mana stays a concept that only exists inside a real match.
- **Dev keys** (`DebugToggle`): F1/F2 raise/lower the Tank's heat by 20; F3 fills the Soldier's Harvest gauge from one random element, ready for Overdrive. Wired into the hub as well as real levels, since the hub is where they're most useful.
- **Display toggles** (`DebugSettings`): enemy health bars, weapon damage numbers and Ritual damage numbers, switched from the pause menu's Options screen. Saved per machine to `user://settings.cfg` with Godot's `ConfigFile`, not in the player save — they're this machine's preferences, not part of anyone's progress. Hiding health bars also updates enemies already alive, since each otherwise reads the flag only on spawn.
- **Floating combat text** is capped at a fixed count via `ActiveRegistry`, oldest evicted first — a zero-cooldown tower with chain, fork and salvo stacked could otherwise keep hundreds alive and cost over 100fps.
- **Players collide with the `Obstacles` layer.** This is a real gameplay rule, not a hub detail: `IceWallSegment` sits on that layer alone, so before this the player could walk through their own wall despite the design always saying otherwise.

**Not built:** a real art pass, and any detail beyond a name in the selection panels — no thumbnails, stats or descriptions.

### Main menu and save profiles

- **Main menu (`MainMenu`):** Play / Settings / Exit — no Singleplayer/Multiplayer split, since multiplayer is just the hub with its lobby open (Section 7, Multiplayer). Settings is the existing Options screen. A plain dark backdrop for now; a lit 3D one is a later polish pass. The pause menu's **Main Menu** button leaves any session on the way — for a host, today that ends the session for everyone else; under host migration (Section 7) the party would carry on without them.
- **The menu is a local overlay, not a level.** It lives in `main.tscn` beside `LevelLoader`, outside the `Level` node, so the level spawner never replicates it; it shows only while no level is loaded, and Play loads the hub.
- **Save profiles (`SaveManager`):** each profile is its own JSON file under `user://profiles/` (Section 7, Save format), with a name and last-played time stored inside it. Play goes straight in on the only profile, to a picker when there are several (name, gold, last played; New and Delete, confirmed with Cancel focused), and to naming a new one when there are none — prefilled "Profile N", so a controller can confirm without typing. **Up to 20 profiles**, a cap to revisit only if someone gives a reason. A save from before profiles becomes "Profile 1", its old file kept beside it as `save_data.tres.bak`. Settings (`settings.cfg`) stay shared across profiles, since they're the machine's preferences, not anyone's progress. A profile's name is only ever shown to its owner — in co-op, other players will see your Steam name. Steam Auto-Cloud can sync `user://profiles/` by configuration alone.
- **Starter weapons:** every profile gets one plain weapon (Common, no mods) per Sentinel, once — equipped if that Sentinel's weapon slot is empty, otherwise put in Inventory (and tried again next load if Inventory is full). Each profile records which Sentinels have had theirs, so a Sentinel added later gets one on the next load and vendoring it is never repeatable.
- **Protecting saves.** Steam Cloud syncs the latest file between machines but keeps no history, so it can't undo a bad save. Each write goes to a temporary file that then replaces the real one, so a crash mid-write leaves the last good save. Every successful load copies the file into that day's backup, overwriting any earlier one from the same day, and the newest 30 daily backups are kept — 30 days _played_, however far apart, so a bug that goes unnoticed for a few days doesn't take every copy with it. A profile that won't load shows in the picker as unplayable, with Restore (newest readable backup; the unreadable file is set aside, not replaced) and Delete; it's never silently replaced. Delete sends a profile and its backups to the recycle bin rather than erasing them. Backups from before the JSON format still restore.
- **Dev flags skip the menu.** `--host`, `--join [address]` and `--profile N` (`LaunchFlags`) boot straight into the hub. `--profile N` plays on profile N, creating it if needed; without it, the last-played profile. **Two instances on one PC need different `--profile` numbers**, or they share a save and the later write wins.
- **Joining needs a profile first (Steam, not built)**, since joining immediately sends the local profile's gear and Sentinel pick to everyone. An invite accepted before one is picked — at the menu, or by launching the game from Steam — follows Play's own rule: straight in on the only profile, the picker first if there are several, naming one first if there are none. An invite accepted inside the picker joins once a profile is chosen.
- **Split-screen means one Steam account can hold several seats**, so a seat's stable identity (Section 7, host migration) is the Steam account plus the local player index, not the account alone.

## 9. Open Questions

### Elemental reactions (design in progress)

**Departs from Genshin's automatic pairwise model.** Towers auto-fire constantly, so a reaction that triggers whenever two elements touch the same enemy becomes ambient background noise rather than a deliberate moment. Reactions instead require a **dedicated consumer** — a specific rune built to trigger off an existing element. Incidental crossfire between two vanilla elemental towers does nothing beyond overwriting the marker.

**Three deliberately distinct concepts:**

- **Status** — single-slot, whichever element hit the enemy most recently. Purely a marker for "is there something here a consumer could trigger off." Overwritten by the next elemental hit whether or not anything consumed it.
- **Ailment** — a named, duration-based elemental effect (Burn: damage over 3s) in the Enemy's debuff list — the elemental kind of debuff (Section 7). Multiple stack and coexist independently, since they don't need to react with each other.
- **Reaction** — what happens when an incoming hit's element meets an existing Status. Always opt-in: it only fires because a rune on the _attacking_ spell checks for it. The Status stays enemy-owned state; the decision to act on it belongs to the rune.

**Reactions are one kind of hit-time rune, not the only kind.** `OnHitRune` is the general hook — a rune that runs the moment a spell's damage lands — and `ReactionRune` is the subset that _spends_ an enemy's Status, which its `OnHit` signals by returning true. `ChillRune` is the counterexample that forced the split: it reads the incoming hit's own element rather than anything already on the enemy, so it must be socketable alongside a reaction rather than competing with it for the same slot. A rune's hook receives an `OnHitContext` carrying the target, the damage packet, the casting spell's own tags and the hit's `Attribution` (so anything the rune starts, like a Burn, is credited to the same player).

**Hit-time runes must not mutate state other hit-time runes read.** Nothing in the loop writes `Status` — it's set once afterward from `DamagePacket.DominantElement` — and `OnHit` takes the damage packet by value, so a rune physically cannot alter the hit's damage (that's what `Rune.DamageMultiplier` is for, at resolve time). Together with the one-reaction-per-Ritual cap those make socket order irrelevant. Break the invariant and order becomes load-bearing, and would need a defined sort rather than inheriting socket order by accident.

**Each reaction is its own Rune**, reusing rune socketing rather than inventing a parallel customization layer — gated by `RequiredTags` and costing a socket, so picking one is a real build tradeoff rather than a settings preference. **A Ritual takes only one reaction rune**, since two would both spend the same Status on the same hit — `ReactionRune` shares one `Rune.ExclusiveGroup` across every reaction, the same mechanism that enforces one-per-type for everything else. This also lets a single pairing have multiple competing rune options later, competing for the same slot, so a new variant is just one more `.tres`.

**Ruled out:** automatic knockback/CC on incidental double-hits (too disruptive too often given automated fire), and elemental _resistance_ (same problem — a passive rule fires on every qualifying hit, not just deliberate ones).

**Wind is consumer-only** and never becomes a Status others can consume, matching Anemo. **Swirl is baked directly into any Wind-dealing spell rather than gated behind a rune** — the one deliberate exception, justified because Wind has no identity outside the reaction system at all, so a Wind spell's entire purpose already _is_ Swirl.

A reaction rune with a disruptive component (knockback, stun) needs its own per-enemy internal cooldown (~5s) so a lingering enemy can't be re-triggered every attack cycle. Pure damage-multiplier reactions don't need one. **Overload** is the named candidate here — Lightning consuming a Fire Status for an AoE knockback burst — and it's precisely why that cooldown matters: it's the reaction that would be unbearable firing on every attack cycle, and viable only when rate-limited. Freeze and Electrocute are the same case. How that cooldown and crowd control in general are limited is its own section, below.

**Goal:** at least one working option for every pairing below. Only Ignition is built; everything else is WIP — named and sketched, not committed. Names are placeholders.

| **Water**             | Scalding Zone _(WIP)_ | —                   | Freeze _(WIP)_  | Electrocute _(WIP)_ | N/A  |
| **Cold**              | Frostburn _(WIP)_     | Freeze _(WIP)_      | —               | Shatter _(WIP)_     | N/A  |

- **Ignition** _(built)_ — Fire and Lightning combust: detonates the latent element into a Burn DoT. Bidirectional (below).
- **Swirl** — baked into every Wind spell rather than gated behind a rune (below). Bonus damage matching the tagged element, spreading that tag to nearby enemies.
- **Blister** _(WIP)_ — Fire consuming Water. A single-use mark consumed by the next non-Fire/non-Water hit for bonus damage.
- **Scalding Zone** _(WIP)_ — Water consuming Fire. A lingering ground patch that slows. It used to share Fire-on-Water with Blister as an undecided either/or; each now has its own cell.
- **Melt** _(WIP)_ — Fire consuming Cold. Deals a percentage of the enemy's max health, less effective against bigger enemies. Needs a per-enemy cooldown, but as a limit on burst rather than lockdown — it's damage, not crowd control, so the crowd-control limits below don't cover it. Designing it without a cooldown (diminishing percent damage, or leaning harder on "less effective against bigger enemies") is the alternative.
- **Frostburn** _(WIP)_ — Cold consuming Fire. Cold damage over time with a slight slow; less damage than Ignition in exchange for the slow.
- **Freeze** _(WIP)_ — Cold and Water, bidirectional. Frozen solid: can't move or attack for a duration. Hard CC, so it's subject to the crowd-control limits below.
- **Electrocute** _(WIP)_ — Water and Lightning, bidirectional. Finds nearby enemies carrying the opposite Status (Water for a Lightning hit, and vice versa) and electrocutes them too: they convulse, unable to move or attack. Shorter than Freeze, as the trade-off for spreading more easily. Hard CC, subject to the limits below.
- **Shatter** _(WIP)_ — Cold and Lightning. Executes an enemy the hit leaves below a health threshold (details below).
- **Overload** _(WIP, no cell)_ — Lightning consuming Fire for an AoE knockback burst. Ignition now fills both Fire/Lightning cells, so Overload would be a competing option for the same pairing rather than the pairing's only rune — which the one-reaction-per-Ritual rule already supports (below).

Freeze and Electrocute are both Water pairings, and should differ on an axis a player can plan around (Roadmap, Section 10) — Freeze trading damage for duration and Electrocute the reverse, or the spread-versus-duration trade above — or the second is strictly worse than the first.

**Another idea for an electric effect, would be amped / electrified where it increases their movement speed. Undecided, and we would need some payoff for speeding them up**

**Shatter (candidate, not built)** — Cold and Lightning meeting executes an enemy the hit leaves below a health threshold (~10%), and does nothing otherwise. A role rather than a damage boost: rarely meaningful against fodder, valuable against tanky enemies, where the last tenth of a big health pool is a lot of damage. It needs no per-enemy cooldown, since an execute isn't disruptive CC.

- **Checked after the hit lands**, so the hit that takes an enemy under the threshold is the one that executes it. Reaction runes currently run _before_ damage is subtracted, and executing from there would break the rule that hit-time runes can't change what the others see, so this needs a small post-damage rune hook rather than being pure data.
- **Bidirectional for now**, like Ignition. Lightning hitting a Cold enemy is the flavorful direction and may end up the only one; the reverse stays unless nothing better claims that cell.
- **Consumes the Status on every attempt**, execute or not — spending it is the cost of trying. Open to reversing if a wasted setup feels bad in play.
- **Bosses are immune or get a reduced threshold.** Bigger, tankier ordinary enemies may get a _higher_ one — they're the intended target.
- Wants a readable payoff — a shatter burst or ice shards — so it doesn't look like an ordinary death. Placeholder VFX until the art pass.

**`IgnitionRune` is built** and is the only bidirectional cell: it fires on Fire-hits-Lightning-Status _and_ the reverse, one rune in two cells, because the two elements combusting reads the same whichever landed second and forcing a different rune per firing order would be busywork. Directionality is per-reaction, not a system rule. **Ignition doesn't spread** — propagation is Swirl's identity, and giving Ignition its own would blur that and hand one rune both a damage reaction and an AoE conversion.

**Per-element damage is `DamagePacket`** (one float per element), not a tag beside a single number — a tag can't express _how much_ of a hit was a given element, which a Lightning-Rod-style Ailment redirecting a percentage of only the Lightning portion would need. `Enemy.Status` tracks `DamagePacket.DominantElement`, excluding Physical and Wind from candidacy.

**Chill is built.** `ChillRune` is an `OnHitRune` that applies a `ChillAilment` (30% slow, 3s) whenever a hit carries Cold damage, giving `Tag.Cold` its first mechanical identity beyond Ice Wall's tag-only one. It's deliberately a rune rather than a baseline property of Cold damage: a baseline would be free, always-on, and would **strobe**, since `Status` is single-slot and auto-firing towers overwrite it several times a second — the same ambient-noise problem that made reactions opt-in. Requiring a socket makes it a build decision and sidesteps the strobe entirely, since an Ailment carries its own duration independent of `Status`.

**Open, and decided together:** a hit-time effect's magnitude should derive from _its own element's_ damage in that hit, never the total — so Ignition's burn should scale off the Fire and Lightning portions combined (both are participants), not off `.Total`, and its trigger should loosen from "that element is dominant" to "that element is present at all," since a player who spent a socket on a reaction should get the payoff. None of that is implemented; Ignition still gates on `DominantElement` and scales off `.Total`. Separately, for **rate-valued** effects like Chill's slow percent — which have no natural damage scale at all — the lean is that a spell _carrying the matching tag_ gets full magnitude regardless of composition, while an off-tag spell dealing that element incidentally gets scaled by its share. That keeps a Blizzard chilling at full strength no matter what gear is bolted onto it, and confines the "adding a foreign element dilutes your own utility" trap to the off-tag case where a reduced payoff is the expected deal. Unreachable until damage conversion or a socket-compatibility override exists, so it's settled in principle only.

**Ground effects are their own family, independent of what spawns them.** A `GroundEffect` is persistent terrain at a location — it knows nothing about spells, so a cast effect, a rune firing off `IceWall.Destroyed`, or a reaction can each drop one in a line. `ChilledGround` is the first. A barrage like Ice Storm is deliberately _not_ one: a storm is a cast in progress rather than terrain, and the only thing the two share is "find the enemies inside a circle," which is a query helper rather than a base class.

**Wet Ground (sketch only, not designed).** A puddle where Lightning damage dealt to anything inside also splashes to everything else inside, at a fraction of the value. Appealing because it gives Water an identity as _terrain_ without needing the enemy-facing status effect that was rejected, and because the conduction fantasy is instantly legible. Mechanically it would be an ailment the area applies, hooking `OnHostDamaged` — the same shape as Health Link's mirror, with the splash never reaching ailments so it can't ping-pong between wet enemies. Because it _should_ set Status (below), it can't reuse `HitType.Mirrored`, which sets none — it would be a new `HitType` that sets Status but skips ailments. Two deliberate differences from Health Link: the splash _should_ set Status, since those enemies really were hit by lightning (which lets a puddle set up Ignition across a group), and it should **not** be true damage, since conduction is new damage rather than a copy of something already mitigated. **Open:** the payoff scales with crowd size, and chokepoints are exactly where a tower defense manufactures crowds — a cap on splash targets is the obvious lever if it gets silly. Needs a Water source to exist at all, so it's unreachable today.

**Shroud** is the first rune that changes how the _Ritual_ is treated rather than what its spell does — enemies neither see it nor collide with it, and it renders semi-transparent to make that obvious. It's the player-side answer to towers constraining placement: rather than removing the threat, you buy freedom from it with a socket. No required tags, so it fits any spell, and the cost is purely the slot it occupies. It needed the `Rituals`/`RitualHitbox` layer split (Section 4) to exist at all, since a shrouded tower must stay shootable and repairable. **Open:** if it turns out to be an auto-include on every tower, the lever is breaking stealth when the tower casts, which keys its value off cooldown and makes a slow spell like Ice Storm a far better host than a fast one.

### Crowd-control limits (design in progress)

Towers fire constantly, so an unlimited hard-CC reaction permanently locks whatever it touches. The problem is broader than any one reaction: the ideas list already holds Freeze, Electrocute, Crystallize, Black Ice, Earthquake, Drown, Tornado and a Chronomancer's Stasis, and a limit that only stops Freeze re-freezing does nothing about **Freeze → Electrocute → Freeze → Earthquake** chaining one enemy into a permanent lock. So there are two layers.

**Layer 1 — per-ailment cooldown (decided).** An enemy can't be put under the same hard-CC ailment again until that ailment's cooldown has passed.

- **The ailment _is_ the cooldown.** A hard-CC ailment like Frozen has two durations, authored separately — e.g. frozen for 2s, then recovering for 6s — rather than one total. After the active part it stays in its slot, **recovering** (`Debuff.IsRecovering`, Section 7): it no longer stops the enemy and doesn't count as present — no ice drawn, not an ailment to anything that strips ailments — but it still occupies the slot. Once the recovering window runs out, it leaves the list as usual. Nothing new has to track "recently frozen."
- **A new Frozen is discarded for as long as one is in the slot — active or recovering.** Frozen overrides `ResolveAgainst` to always discard, like `BurnAilment` overrides it to merge. The default rule is strongest-wins with equal applications refreshing the duration, which for a stun is exactly the permafreeze problem: a tower re-freezing every half second would keep resetting the active timer forever.
- **Keyed by ailment, not by rune.** Every source of a Frozen shares one cooldown — two different reaction runes that both freeze (Freeze sits in two cells of the reaction table, as does Electrocute), or a spell and a rune. Keying by rune would let those take turns. It also rides the existing one-per-class slot rule, so it needs no new slot machinery.
- **Per enemy, shared across every tower** — not per tower. Five towers each holding their own timer would take turns and permafreeze a lane.
- **Durations arrive with the ailment.** Like every debuff's numbers (Section 7, Debuff), the active and immunity durations are handed in by whatever applies it — a reaction rune exports them the way `IgnitionRune` exports `BurnDuration`, and can scale them by tier. A `Rune` is a shared `.tres`, so it could never hold the running timer itself anyway.
- **A blocked reaction still consumes the Status.** Spending it is an additional cost on top of the reaction's power. The rune knows it was blocked because `ApplyDebuff` already returns false when an incoming debuff loses to the one in its slot. Consequence: Status is shared by every tower hitting an enemy, so a reaction blocked by immunity still eats a Status another tower's reaction wanted — reaction towers compete over the same enemies, which pushes players to space them out.
- **No multiplayer timing problem.** Rune on-hits only ever run on the host (`Enemy.ReceiveForwardedHit`: only Rituals carry runes, and the host runs every Ritual), so every check reads the host's live state.
- **Doesn't cover non-CC reactions.** Melt's per-enemy limit is about burst damage, not lockdown, and it applies no ailment to hold an immunity phase. Its limiter is open (see Melt above).

**Layer 2 — tenacity (leaning, not decided).** A per-enemy CC-resistance value covering every CC source — reactions, spells and Sentinel abilities alike.

- **It builds from time actually spent under CC, not from applications.** A fast tower re-freezing an already-frozen enemy adds nothing; only the seconds spent locked down count. Building it per application was rejected, since a fast-firing tower would max it out within a second.
- **It decays over time**, and **CC durations shrink as it rises**: the first freeze lands in full, a quick follow-up gets less, one after that barely registers. It shortens only the active part of a CC ailment; the recovering window after it stays as authored.
- **Base tenacity comes from the enemy's weight class** — fodder none, heavies some, bosses a lot — rather than flat CC immunities, which the enemy philosophy rules out (Section 5) and which are dull regardless. Nothing is ever fully immune; bigger things just stay locked down for less time. It generalizes the mass-based pushback already sketched for spells like Tsunami, and could replace per-effect boss rules (Shatter's threshold, a max-health clamp) with one number.
- **Slows are unlimited.** Permanent slow is a genre staple and already bounded by slows stacking multiplicatively; only hard CC (stuns, roots, freezes, knockdowns) feeds tenacity.
- **The counter-argument, noted:** chaining CC already costs several towers and a socket each, so it's arguably a build worth allowing. Tenacity is then a tuning knob rather than a wall.
- Tenacity needs "is this enemy under any hard CC?" — a virtual `IsHardCC` on each debuff, added when tenacity is built (Section 7, Debuff). A recovering one doesn't count.

### Triggers (design in progress)

**Triggers are authored, never assembled.** PoE's cast-on-crit and cast-when-damaged are a great archetype, but letting players pair any trigger with any payload removes too many constraints to balance.

- **Shelved: runes that carry or fire a second spell** — a Fireball releasing a Lightning Bolt from where it lands. Whether the payload takes the Ritual's other runes, how its damage is cut and how the UI shows that are all unsolved, and multiplying with Salvo and Chain is the balance risk.
- **Free-form triggers come from uniques** — "your Lightning Rituals call a strike on every enemy they hit." A unique is one designed effect, not a combinatorial space, and gear reaching into towers isn't new: Rituals already resolve against their owner's sheet. Waits on uniques existing (Section 6).
- **Out: cast when damaged.** Rituals fire on their own anyway, so the only new thing is a reason to let a tower take hits and babysit its repairs for damage, which feels bad.
- **Maybe: a crit-triggered Ritual** that does nothing until a nearby player crits, then fires at what was crit — cast-on-crit in a form that suits towers. Not designed.

**Event runes fit:** runes reacting to something that happened — a kill, a wall breaking, an enemy dying nearby. Whether a rune hears only its own Ritual's events ("this Ritual killed a frozen enemy") or anything in range ("an enemy died nearby", "a Ritual in range broke") is decided per rune, like a reaction's direction. Candidates: shattering a frozen enemy this Ritual kills, Splinter burst, a broken wall exploding. **All of them need a rune hook that runs after damage lands, or on a kill** — runes today only run at hit time, before damage is subtracted. It's the same hook the Shatter reaction needs, and the first code to read `Hit.Attribution` for kill credit.

### Rune ideas not yet built

Every rune should hook a _tag_, not a spell class, so it applies to any future spell sharing that tag. Worth checking each idea against that explicitly. Speculative spell, rune and character drafts live in `ideas.md`, not here.

- **Prime** — a "Primed" debuff; the next `Tag.Lightning` hit on a Primed enemy sparks a random _nearby_ enemy, evaluated fresh off the struck enemy's position so no group tracking is needed. **Must be capped to once per cast** — without that it compounds with `ChainRune` worse than Fireball's Chain+Explode ceiling, which is already bounded to one shot.
- **Targeting runes** — overriding a spell's default priority. Unblocked; `TargetPriority` already exists as one field. **Targeting costs a socket**, not a free per-Ritual toggle — the default (`ClosestToGoal`) should be good enough most of the time. To make that socket a real trade rather than a tax, a targeting rune also carries a damage bonus. Candidates in `ideas.md`: highest health (current or max), lowest health / killing blow, flying, closest to or furthest from the Ritual, furthest from the objective, enemies carrying a Status, and firing in a fixed direction.
- **Manual activation** — the Ritual never casts on its own; any player walks up and presses interact to fire it. 100% more damage, as a placeholder. Cooldown becomes whichever is greater of double it or it plus 5s, so a spell with none gets 5s, a 2s one 7s and an 8s one 16s. That isn't a percentage, so it's a step after the rest of cooldown resolution, before cooldown-recovery gear, which then speeds up the added time like any other. Pressing it with no enemy in range wastes the cast — you walked over and chose to. Lets a long-cooldown spell like Cataclysm wait for the moment it's needed.
- **Resonance** and **Far Resonance** — the Ritual never casts on its own; it casts whenever any Ritual within the rune's resonance radius casts, any player's included. A damage bonus pays for the lost uptime, size open. Far Resonance also extends spell range, so it reaches its source's target more often.
  - **It aims at its source's target**: the in-range enemy nearest it, or for a ground-targeted spell the in-range point nearest it; a projectile fires toward an out-of-range target and flies as far as it goes. This is what makes it more than a worse tower — a Fire Ritual plus a Resonant Ignition Ritual becomes a deliberate pair hitting the same enemy.
  - With no enemy in range it fires anyway and goes on cooldown. To revisit if that feels bad.
  - **Its own cooldown still applies**, and a pulse arriving during cooldown is dropped rather than queued. A Resonant cast counts as a cast, so chains cascade across a cluster, bounded because each Ritual fires at most once per cooldown.
  - Incompatible with spells carrying their own `CastTrigger` (Ice Wall).
  - **Radius and range are separate.** The radius is Ritual to Ritual, fixed on the rune, and shows during placement as an outline ring, distinct from the filled range circle, with a light translucent line to each Ritual already inside it. The lines also show when looking at a placed Resonant Ritual.
  - **Built together with manual activation:** both are a Ritual that casts only when pulsed — by a player's interact or a neighbor's cast — so one path serves both, and a manual Ritual at the center of a Resonant cluster fires the whole cluster on one press.
  - Host-only like all Ritual logic: a casting Ritual looks up Resonant Rituals in range through `LevelContext.Rituals` and pulses them. The pending pulse lives on the Ritual, never on the shared rune or the rebuilt context.

### Parked topics

Raised and deliberately set aside for their own conversations, so they aren't lost:

- **Enemy collision.** Solid enemies make CC spill: freezing the front three enemies of a column effectively freezes everyone queued behind them. Letting players build a kill box is fine — tower defense encourages it — so this reopens the rejected idea of disabling enemy-vs-enemy collision (Section 4, Ice Wall). Shapes on the table: no enemy collision, with AoE damage scaling down past some number of targets (square-root scaling, as WoW does); soft separation, where enemies push apart for spacing but can squeeze past each other; a hybrid where frozen, stunned or rooted enemies stop being solid so the queue flows around them; or a mix. Touches movement, AoE balance and the packed-enemy jitter at walls.
- **Rune colors**, in the spirit of PoE's gem colors. Colors already carry two meanings in this UI — element (`DamageTypeColors`, on spells, tokens and the Harvest gauge) and rarity (`RarityColors`, on rune tiles) — so a third system needs its own visual channel (e.g. rarity on the border, category on the fill). Also open: whether a color is only a signal, or a socketing rule the way PoE's are.
- **Growing the tag vocabulary.** Today's tags (Projectile, AoE, Duration, Trigger, Chaining, Wall) can't tell a line from a cone from a field, so a rune can't target those shapes. The drafted spells suggest Line, Cone, Field, Pulse, Traveling and Support. It also feeds rune grouping, if runes end up filtered by tag.
- **Damage-over-time hits set no Status** (`HitType.DamageOverTime`). A ground effect meant to keep re-applying a Status (a Magma Floes feeding a delayed double-hit like Hydro Blade), or a zero-damage Status-setter like Rain, needs another way to set one.

### Undecided design

- **Full scope of the elemental tag system** — more elements, and how conversion runes work.
- **Damage roll ranges** (PoE-style min/max per hit) vs. flat deterministic damage. The existing multiplicative stacking already makes exact breakpoints hard to hand-calculate, which weakens the "ranges hurt planning" argument against them.
- **Mana/capacity cost rounding.** Costs stay float through rune resolution and `EconomyManager` converts at the last step, currently rounding _up_, so 10.2 always costs 11 and never rounds down to a free fraction. Ceiling felt safer but isn't committed — revisit once real per-rune multipliers replace the flat test value.
- **Equipment mod pool** — contents, weighting, drop sourcing.
- **PoE-style currency conversion for high-difficulty loot pressure.** Drop fewer raw items on harder content and convert some into stackable crafting currency, so a player buried in duplicates can target-craft instead. Open problem: drops roll per-kill with no signal for "are we in harder content," so this needs an ambient difficulty scalar threaded down to the roll.
- **Item nameplates cluster unreadably at chokepoints.** Overlap avoidance was deliberately dropped because a distance-sorted version made labels reshuffle as the player approached, which was worse. But towers concentrate deaths into a few spots, so plates end up fully stacked and individually untargetable. A stable identity-based ordering rather than a continuously-recomputed one is the likely direction, untried. **Partly defused** by the loot-timing direction — if most drops stop rendering mid-combat, only elite and goblin drops remain to clutter. That defusal would be lost in the regular game if hardcore (Section 2) ships, and the nameplates' presentation isn't settled beyond the clustering either — how loot displays in the world is open as a whole.
- **Per-enemy visual markers don't read at the enemy density levels actually reach.** Both Weak-Point Visor's markers and Health Link's spikes mark individual enemies in a crowd, and picking one marked enemy out of a live wave is genuinely hard — hard enough that verifying the spike placement by eye was a problem in itself. Making the markers bigger or brighter fights the crowd rather than solving it. The likelier direction is CC that thins or slows the crowd so individual enemies are legible at all, plus leaning on cues that read through chaos (a marked enemy moving differently) over cues that need to be visually picked out.
- **Boss-tier enemies.** The time-travel premise motivates a boss archetype, but how a boss fight works mechanically in a lane-based TD isn't started.
- **Where the hub sits in time** — outside time entirely, or anchored to one era. Per-era content scope is entirely TBD and not a slice concern.
- **Multiplayer transport, and what consoles do about it.** The intended shape is Steam peer-to-peer with one player hosting, which fits both the invite-a-friend model and the host-authoritative architecture in Section 7. Consoles have no equivalent of that path, and local split-screen is the obvious answer there — but it's a substantial piece of work rather than a small addition: a second viewport and camera, per-player scoping for every fullscreen panel (the build overlay especially), input routing per seat, and each local player picking their own save profile (Section 8). Likely to happen, not yet scheduled; worth its own conversation before anything commits to it.
- **Low-poly art style and visual identity** — not urgent, but will matter for silhouette readability.

### Not implemented yet

- **Wave-end safety net** — a wave should auto-end after a timeout even if an enemy is stuck on the navmesh.
- **Navmesh re-baking on tower placement.** Placed Rituals have solid collision, but the navmesh only bakes at level load, so enemies won't route around anything placed after. The idea is to re-bake once at the end of a build phase and once when a token deploys mid-wave — not per placement (expensive) and not never (broken pathing). Note this is distinct from the wall-rebaking dead end in Section 4.

### Rune and spell iconography

Undecided between two directions, and not needed for the slice.

- **Procedural sigils** — anchor points evenly spaced on a circle; a name's letters map to points connected in sequence, generating a glyph per rune without hand-drawing one. Socketing overlays each rune's glyph onto the spell's, so a built tower's sigil is a compound. **A letter-based prototype was built and found lacking:** repeated letters still look cluttered even deduped, and short words often don't self-intersect, reading as a plain polygon. The technique it was based on (chaos-magic word sigils) always involved hand-restyling the plotted skeleton afterward, so raw procedural output was probably never going to look finished.
- **Functional iconography** — a small fixed set of primitive strokes tied to mechanical tags, composed per rune from the tags it touches. The player can learn to read a build at a glance instead of memorizing shapes, and primitives are reused across every rune sharing a tag rather than authored per rune. A tag-based prototype exists (anchor points are `Tag` values, so point count is designer-controlled rather than dictated by English spelling) and renders 4 candidate orderings side by side so a shape can be picked by eye. **Coded but never run or visually evaluated.**
- **Anchor count tradeoff:** vocabulary size should come from counting what's needed, not picked as a round number. More points sit closer together angularly, and past somewhere around 10–16 near-neighbor connections start reading as noise. Vocabulary size and per-glyph clutter are different knobs — a bigger vocabulary means more distinct shapes across the roster, not a busier glyph. Adding a point later reflows every glyph's angles, so reserve a few unused slots before locking this at ship time.

### Deferred cleanups

Looked at and deliberately not done, recorded so they aren't re-discovered from scratch:

- **`Spell.Tags`/`Rune.RequiredTags` allocate a fresh `List<Tag>` on every read** (expression-bodied, and `IsCompatibleWith` reads both). Should be `static readonly Tag[]`. Mechanical, ~20 files, no behavior change — it's UI-time code, not per-frame.
- **`EnemyTargeting`'s local functions capture `fromPosition`**, heap-allocating a closure and delegate per call. Roughly 150 tiny short-lived objects a second at ten rituals, which gen-0 absorbs without noticing. Removing it means an enum switch inside the loop, which reads worse for no measurable gain.
- **`ArcVisual` spawns two nodes plus a timer per segment, per arc**, and a forked chained Lightning Bolt emits a dozen-plus arcs per cast continuously. Real churn, but the whole effect is placeholder VFX — the fix (a `MultiMeshInstance3D` or pooled line renderer) belongs with the art pass.

## 10. Roadmap

The vertical slice is complete: place towers, survive waves, win or lose, with gold, items, equipment mods, a working elemental reaction, tower disable/repair, tokens, a ranged enemy, and the hub's crafting table and vendor all real.

**Current focus: the radial construction menu** (Section 4). Built: every wheel, the Ritual Inscription, favorites (per-spell rings and the pinned wheel), token authoring and the spiral. Left, roughly in order: the **Arrange screen** (reorder, rename, delete and pin favorites by hand — which also enables "taking charge" of the pinned wheel), then **sort modes** and the **which-wheel-opens-first setting**. The temporary rune buckets wait on a larger draft of rune ideas (`ideas.md`). Hard CC's debuff groundwork (`IsRecovering`, Section 7) is designed but not built.

Next, in order:

1. **Sentinel ability kits.** The `Ability` framework, four-slot array, tap/hold, input actions, cooldown resolution, the `AbilityBar` HUD row and the gauge pill all exist, and Soldier's whole kit is built on top. Tank's kit and the crosshair cues are built too.
2. **New spells.** Ice Storm is built (Section 4), which gave Cold a damage spell and made Chill reachable. A second bombardment — a meteor shower — is now pure content, since `Bombardment` carries no element of its own.
3. **Hard CC — Frozen and Electrocuted.** Both are Water pairings, both need `PreventsAction` threaded through `WalkingEnemy`'s target state machine, and both need the per-enemy internal cooldown (Section 9, Crowd-control limits) — on the **enemy**, not on the rune, or five towers each carrying their own timer permafreeze a lane. The ailment _is_ the cooldown: a hard-CC ailment stays in its slot, recovering (`Debuff.IsRecovering`, Section 7), after its active duration ends, so nothing new has to track "recently frozen." They should differ on an axis a player can plan around — Frozen trading damage for duration, Electrocuted the reverse — or the second one is strictly worse than the first. **Open:** a stopped enemy is an obstacle, and what nav-agent avoidance does when the ones behind it pile up is unknown until it's tried.
4. **New runes**, each authored with its full set of tiers (Section 4, Rune acquisition).
5. **New enemies** — a Flyer is the leading candidate, which is also when the "is movement style its own reusable axis" question (Section 5) needs a real answer.
6. **A new level.**
7. **Meta-progression — someday, not near-term.** Persistent Architect XP, tied to difficulty cleared rather than matches played, so grinding easy content can't outpace real progress. `PlayerSaveData` already carries `Level` and `Xp` fields and nothing reads them yet. The track would unlock new base Spells, and match rewards would scale with performance — objective health remaining, waves survived cleanly, time taken — instead of today's flat victory bonus. **Guardrail:** none of it may ever let player power outstrip the towers'; they stay the dominant source of defensive strength. Note this unlocks spells and progression only, never gear — the Architect has no equipment of his own (Section 3), so the old "XP unlocks an item slot for him" idea is dead.

Worth doing alongside these rather than blocking them, since more enemies and levels make both more likely to get hit: the wave-end safety-net timeout, and navmesh re-baking on tower placement.
