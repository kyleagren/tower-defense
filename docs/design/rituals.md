# Rituals

A base skill (**Spell**, e.g. Fireball) is an immutable data definition. Modifiers (**Runes**) transform that definition or hook into its events. Composition, not subclassing.

**Tags** (Projectile, AoE, Duration, Trigger, Traveling, Line, elemental types) let a Rune apply generically to "anything with tag X" rather than needing per-spell logic. Elemental tags exist for build-crafting depth only — enemies have no elemental resistances by design ([enemies.md](enemies.md)).

Elements so far: **Fire, Lightning, Cold, Wind, Water.** Each has one color everywhere (`DamageTypeColors`) — damage numbers, Status tints, spell wedges, tokens and lightning visuals alike: Fire orange, Water blue, Cold pale blue, Lightning a pale electric violet (blue being taken), Wind green, and Physical tan. A damage number takes its hit's dominant element, or Wind's green when Wind is the hit's largest share (Physical included) — Wind never becomes a Status, so dominance alone leaves it out. Runes socket into 4 slots per tower, one per corner. Towers don't level or scale within a match, and duplicate rune types on one Spell are rejected in code.

**Rune roster and naming.** Every rune's display name is one evocative word rather than a mechanical description, the way ARPGs typically name modifiers: **Haste** (cast speed), **Tempo** (cooldown recovery), **Extend** (range), **Velocity** (projectile and traveling speed), **Salvo** (multi-projectile), **Expand** (increased area), plus **Chain**, **Fork**, **Pierce**, **Nova**, **Return**, and the **Ignition** reaction rune. Targeting runes follow suit — **Proximity**, **Giantslayer**, **Cull**, **Swarm**, **Embersight**, **Bearing**, **Anchor** — while their classes keep descriptive names (`ClosestEnemyTargetingRune`).

**Mana/capacity cost is multiplicative, PoE-support-style.** Every `Rune` has a `ManaCostMultiplier` applied automatically during resolve, multiplying the tower's resolved `ManaCost` in the same `base * (1 + increased) * more` shape as Damage/Range. Every rune currently uses a flat placeholder 1.2× for testing the mechanism; `MaxCapacity` is a placeholder 1000 to leave room to experiment before real numbers are chosen.

**Targeting.** `TargetPriority` is a flat field on `SpellCastContext`, seeded from `Spell.BaseTargetPriority`, overridden by a targeting rune ([runes.md](runes.md)) and read by `EnemyTargeting.FindTarget`. The default is `ClosestToGoal`, ranked by remaining nav-path length, which is comparable across lanes because every lane converges on the same objective. A spell can set its own: Tesla Coil's is `Random`, any enemy in range, rolled fresh on every search. Range is always measured from the ritual; only the choice among in-range enemies varies. Chain jumps, forks and projectile redirects deliberately stay nearest-first, matching what those runes promise. **Player presence is never a targeting fallback** — multiple lanes mean the player can only be in one place.

**Interaction model.** Placement and aiming use a center-screen reticle plus a raycast into the world, not a screen-space cursor. This keeps mouse and controller unified under one system; full controller support is a requirement, not an afterthought ([controls_and_ui.md](controls_and_ui.md)).

**Where a Ritual can stand.** The ghost is blocked if its collision cylinder cuts into anything on Obstacles, PlacementBlocked or another Ritual's hitbox — tested 5cm above where it stands (`RitualPlacer.PlacementClearance`), so the surface it rests on doesn't count. Level geometry is World and Obstacles at once (a roof's top is floor, its sides are wall, one body either way), so touching isn't overlapping. A slope steeper than about 6% across the Ritual's width still cuts in uphill, so stairs and ramps stay blocked.

Player-buff towers (a "Buff Totem") buff the player instead of attacking, range-based so the player must stand near it, and cost capacity like anything else.

**Reading a Ritual at a glance.** The outer ring of the rune circle on the ground fills toward the next cast, dimmed, and lights up full when ready; the core brightens as it fills. Only a Ritual whose spell has a cooldown (runes included) tracks this; on fast Rituals it would just flicker, so theirs reads full whenever they aren't broken. It's empty while the Ritual is broken or an ongoing effect stands. The host sends the time to the next cast on each cast and every copy counts down itself, so someone joining mid-match sees "ready" until the next cast. Holding Alt (`advanced_tooltip`) while looking at a Ritual shows its range circle, the same one placement shows, from up to 60m (`RitualRangeViewer.ViewRange`) — possibly too far; a short reach is the alternative.

**Ritual details** (`RitualDetailsPanel`), top-left under the phase bar while Alt-looking at a Ritual, answers whether it's earning its keep: its spell and socketed runes, then **Total** and **Wave** columns (Wave is the current wave in combat, the last one during Build) for effective damage, overkill, kills and casts, one row per rune that counts its own effect (Chill's enemies chilled, each reaction's triggers — only when it actually took effect), and damage per mana (effective damage over its cost plus every repair a Sentinel paid). Counted on the host as hits land (`RitualStats`, credited through the hit's attribution, Health Link copies excluded); a joiner's panel asks the host for a snapshot twice a second while it's open, and nothing is sent otherwise.

**Line of sight.** Only terrain blocks it — Ice Walls, Rituals, players and enemies never do (`LineOfSight`, one ray against the World layer).

- **Rituals** only target what they can see from their spawn point; range is still measured from the base. Sight is checked last, and only for a candidate that would win, so a search costs few raycasts. Every chain and fork jump needs sight from the enemy it leaves. Bombardments need it too — a targeting rune that picks a spot could lift that later.
- **Soldier's rifle and Overdrive** stop at terrain and flash there; Overdrive's pierce ends at a wall.
- **Ranged enemies** don't fire without a clear shot: they close in along their path until they have one, and their projectiles stop on terrain.
- **Not covered:** melee and radius effects (explosions, Immolate, fields) still reach through terrain.

**Visual direction.** Every tower is fundamentally magical, so the physical shape needn't change per spell. A Ritual (`RitualVisual`) is a floating rock with a few fragments drifting around it, an energy core above, crystals orbiting the core, Elder Futhark symbols orbiting on a tilted ring further out, a rune circle turning on the ground and the element's particles. Everything takes the spell's element color.

- **The element decides the look** (`RitualAppearance`, one `.tres` per element under `rituals/appearances/`): core turbulence and flow, crystal count, shape, size and motion, the circle's spin, flicker and glyph count, the symbols' orbit, and the particles' motion. A fire Ritual gets a churning core and fast angular crystals; a cold one slow, long crystals. The spell itself adds only its glyph, floating in the core (`Spell.Glyph`, drawn in Noto Sans Runic).
- **Each Ritual is a little different:** crystal lengths and tilts, rock shapes and every bob's timing are rolled at placement, so a row of the same build doesn't read as copies.
- **Breaking drains it.** Over 0.6s the colors go dark, the motion coasts to a stop, the crystals sink, the rocks settle to the ground and the particles stop; repair runs it back.
- **Purely cosmetic:** every machine builds and animates its own, off state the Ritual already syncs.
- A placement ghost shows only the bare rune circle, in the preview color.

## Rune acquisition

Runes carry the same `Rarity` axis Items use. **Common is unlocked for every rune from the start** so a new player always has real options; higher tiers must be found. Storage is one `.tres` per rarity tier per rune type — accepted as authoring overhead rather than re-plumbing `SpellInstance`/`Ritual.RuneSlots`/`BuildMenu`/`RitualPlacer` to carry a tier dimension everywhere a bare `Rune` reference currently flows. Each type gets a subfolder under `assets/runes/`, and `RuneCatalog` scans recursively.

- **Drops** come through `LootGenerator.GenerateRandomDrop()` like gear, but roll `Rarity` from their own steeper table — Uncommon 100, Rare 30, Epic 8, Legendary 1, no Common since it's always known — since a tier is a permanent unlock rather than one item. Then pick uniformly among that rarity's not-yet-known runes. A rarity with nothing left to teach is excluded from the roll; if everything is known, it falls back to a gear roll rather than wasting itself.
- **A drop is a `RuneTome : Item`**, reusing world-item pickup, nameplates and tooltips entirely. It sits in Inventory until the player right-clicks it (`inventory_use`, Square on a controller) to learn it.
- **Cascade-unlock:** learning a higher tier grants every lower not-yet-known tier of the same rune too, so owned tiers are always a contiguous range from Common upward. This is what keeps the tier-picker UI simple — nothing to grey out, only ever list what's owned.
- **Tier values.** Each tier steps one number: a bonus grows (Expand, Velocity and Extend 25% → 45% in 5% steps; Haste and Tempo 15% → 35%; Chill's slow 25% → 37% in 3% steps; Pierce 2 → 6), or a damage penalty shrinks by 0.05 per tier without ever reaching zero (Salvo and Chain 0.75 → 0.95, Fork and Return 0.55 → 0.75, Nova 0.35 → 0.55). Reaction runes get about 5% stronger per tier (Ignition's Burn 50% → 60% of the hit, Frostburn 35% → 42%, Overload's burst 40% → 48%, Melt 12% → 15% of max health, Shatter's threshold 10% → 12%, Freeze 2s → 2.4s and Electrocute 1s → 1.2s of active time). Trigger runes scale their damage bonus the same way (Manual Activation 100% → 120% more, Resonance 30% → 36%, Far Resonance 25% → 30%, Fast Resonance 8% → 9.6%). Legendary is roughly 1.5–2× a Common's bonus; Common bases sit a step low so higher tiers have room. **Shroud is Common-only** — it's on or off, with nothing to scale. **A new rune ships with all its tiers.**

**Per-copy scarcity (framework only).** Independent of rarity, a known rune can be limited-quantity (`RuneInventoryEntry.Unlimited = false`). `Quantity` is the total ever owned and is never decremented; "available right now" (`RuneCatalog.IsAvailable`) is derived live by scanning currently-placed Rituals' `RuneSlots`. This matters: an earlier spend-and-refund counter was scrapped because any path skipping `Ritual.Destroy` — a crash included — permanently lost a copy. Deriving availability can't desync. Nothing currently grants a limited entry, so this is inert. **Tokens can never use a limited rune**, since a token's loadout locks in during the unpressured Planning phase, exactly when a scarce rune should go on a real tower instead. Favorites may reference one and simply show unavailable when out of copies.

## Damage & repair

**A Ritual sits on two physics layers, and the split is load-bearing.** `Rituals` means _blocks movement_ — it's what enemies and the player collide with. `RitualHitbox` means _everything else a Ritual's body is for_: being shot by enemy projectiles, being raycast by repair and destroy, and blocking placement of another tower on the same spot. They were one layer until the **Shroud** rune needed a tower that enemies walk through but can still be hit and repaired, which is impossible with a single bit — clearing it takes the hits and the repairs with it. Shroud clears `Rituals` alone. Consequence to remember: a mask that wants "is there a tower here" wants `RitualHitbox`, and only movement collision should ever reference `Rituals`.

A damaged tower is normally **disabled, not destroyed**, and is repaired by funneling mana in — free during Build, so between waves. True destruction is rare and returns attached runes to inventory, never losing them. The **Sapper** is the enemy that meaningfully threatens towers — a visible, prioritizable threat rather than universal collateral risk. Disabled towers show on the minimap and visibly go dark (Visual direction, above).

Repair and destroy both use the same reticle raycast as placement, capped to a similar interaction range, with a contextual world-space prompt near the tower. **Repair and destroy are both hold-to-confirm (~1s)**, each with a progress indicator on the same prompt, cancelling if released early or if the raycast loses the tower. Repair's cost shows on its own line under the prompt, tinted red when unaffordable, and only while the tower is actually damaged. Chosen over a persistent mode toggle specifically to avoid mode-confusion on the harder-to-reverse action.

**Repair and refund pricing share one rule: the missing-health fraction times `GameRules.RepairCostFraction` (0.5).** A full repair of a broken tower costs half the tower's _own_ cost at its owner's current multipliers — placement extras like an Ice Wall's segments are left out, since the tower's health doesn't scale with them. Destroying refunds the mana actually paid, scaled down by the same fraction: everything back at full health, half back when broken, rounded down. So a Sentinel tearing down a broken tower to rebuild it is never a way around repairing it. **Build (and the hub) is exempt from both:** repairs are free and every refund is in full, so between waves damage costs nothing to put right. The host accepts a repair or destroy only from a seated player. Capacity always comes back in full — it's a ceiling on what can be built, not a currency, and shorting it would lose it for the rest of the match.

## Construction & deployment UI

`Q` drives two flows that behave nothing alike, built as separate systems because they sit under opposite time pressure, and the phase picks which: the construction menu in Build and the hub, token pick mode in Combat. On a controller they split across two buttons: Triangle for the construction menu, R1 for tokens.

**Any player, Build phase — construction menu.** `Q` (Triangle) opens `BuildMenu`, a full-screen radial menu (design below): an element ring, then that element's spells, then the Ritual Inscription where runes are socketed, then placement. No time pressure, so this is a rich browsing experience. It stands the character still, keyboard included, and closes itself when the phase changes.

- **Built so far:** the element ring (Favorites at the top, then Fire, Lightning, Cold, Water, Wind, Physical and Multi-Element clockwise), each element's spell ring, each spell's favorites ring, the pinned favorites wheel, the Ritual Inscription with its rune bucket ring, rune ring and tier wheel, Save Favorite, token authoring through the token wheel, and the spiral for rings past 8 items. On the wheels the center shows the highlighted item's name and its cost against current mana, red when unaffordable; the description sits below the wheel on its own dark panel, hidden when empty.
- **Layout.** No dimmed backdrop — the world stays visible. The wheel sits at a fixed anchor up and left of screen center (40% across, 42% down, to be tuned) for mouse and controller alike, keeping the reticle, and the spot about to be built on, uncovered. It can't open at the cursor, since the mouse is captured during play and has no position of its own. Text over the world or over wedge colors (which run from dark to near-white) carries a black outline. Circle, Escape or right-click steps back a ring, and closes the menu from the element ring; `Q` (Triangle), the key that opened it, closes it from any ring.
- **Arranging by hand** — spell order, favorites (reorder, rename, delete, pin) and rune order — happens on the Organize panel, below.
- **Deferred until there are many more spells and runes:** sort modes and the which-wheel-opens-first setting.
- **Socketing rules:** socket state survives backing out and returning for the same spell, even across closing the menu, clearing only when a different spell is picked. A rune that conflicts with one already socketed on the same spell — the same type, or a second reaction rune — or that's unavailable shows dimmed in place and can't be picked, making the rule visible instead of silently rejecting it. Deliberate tier downgrades are intended, e.g. socketing a Common damage rune to land on an exact breakpoint another tower can finish. Clearing a whole near-finished build takes a held press, so a stray press can't wipe it. Rune description text lives on the `Rune` resource, not in UI code.
- Renders as a blocking overlay over the opening player's view. Since co-op has both players building simultaneously, a true game pause won't work — the overlay needs per-player scoping, mechanism still open.

**Favorites.** A saved spell plus up to 4 exact-tier `Rune` references. Picking one goes straight to placement — it's a locked stamp, not a pre-filled starting point. Where favorites are saved and picked in the radial menu is in its design below.

- **A tap saves instantly under a default name**: the first socketed rune — top-left, then clockwise — then the spell, "Shroud Fireball", or just the spell's name with nothing socketed. Collisions get a suffix, "Shroud Fireball (1)". Renaming happens on the Organize panel.
- **Holding** Save Favorite opens a name field under the wheel instead, pre-filled with that default and selected; Enter saves, Escape cancels, an empty name falls back to the default, and the wheel ignores input while it's open. On a controller the hold still opens it, typed on the keyboard.
- **Saving an exact duplicate is refused** — same spell, same runes at the same tiers, in any sockets, since socket order doesn't change what a ritual does — and the inscription names the favorite that already holds it.

**Tokens.** Up to 3 held unused at once — a "how many can sit ready" cap, not a per-wave cast limit. Crafting and recrafting are Build-phase-only and cost nothing up front; mana and capacity are spent only at deployment, specifically so a token shores up a defense that didn't quite get finished rather than becoming a second economy to budget during prep. Authoring happens in the construction menu (the token wheel, below), with a gold ring around every wheel and a gold "Token N" tag marking the menu the whole time, so it's never ambiguous that confirming writes to a token instead of starting placement.

- **Tokens can be authored straight from the field.** During Build and in the hub, R1 with d-pad left/up/right — exactly as when deploying — opens the build menu straight into authoring that token. Keyboard 1/2/3 stay the Sentinel's abilities outside the menu, so on a keyboard tokens are authored from inside it, where 1/2/3 jump straight to a token.

**Any player, mid-wave — token deployment.** Combat only; the hub follows Build's rules and never deploys. `Q` toggles a lightweight pick mode that remaps `1`/`2`/`3` to the player's tokens. Right-click, `Q`, or Escape all back out. On a controller, pick mode lasts exactly as long as R1 is held, with d-pad left/up/right as the three tokens. Pressing a mapped number drops into the same ghost/reticle placement a real tower uses; confirming spends live and consumes the token, cancelling doesn't. No browsing, no sockets — optimized for speed under pressure. Tokens are consumable: destroying a tower built from one doesn't give it back.

**Editing and copying a placed Ritual (designed, not built).** Both are Build and hub only, and both start from the same step: reading a placed Ritual's spell and runes into the build menu's in-progress build. In Combat they're off, which keeps Build the phase for considered decisions and tokens the only answer under pressure.

- **Edit** opens the Ritual Inscription on the Ritual in view, within repair reach, already filled in with its runes. The spell can't change: a different spell means a different footprint, so that's destroying it and placing a new one. The bottom action becomes **Apply**, charging or refunding only the cost difference.
  - **It adds no power, only convenience.** Build refunds in full and capacity always comes back, so tearing a Ritual down and rebuilding it was already free; editing saves the walk through the menu and keeps the exact spot.
  - **Applied by replacing the Ritual**: the old one goes and a new one with the edited runes spawns at the same position and rotation, keeping its placement data (an Ice Wall's endpoints, Bearing's direction, Anchor's spot). A fresh Ritual can't carry stale state — cooldowns, a standing Ice Wall, Shroud's layers. Adding Bearing or Anchor runs its aim step after Apply.
  - **Anyone can edit any Ritual, and the original placer stays its owner**, so its cost and scaling still come from the owner's gear. The editor sockets only runes they own themselves. Accepted knowingly: someone else's edit can change what a player's Ritual does, and whose limited-copy rune it holds is open.
  - The Ritual's own runes count as available while editing it, so keeping a limited-copy rune it already holds isn't refused.
- **Copy** — the eyedropper. Pressed while looking at any Ritual, any player's, it loads that build and goes straight to placement, like picking a favorite. Cancelling placement leaves the build in the inscription to tweak, save as a favorite or as a token.
  - **Partial copies are fine.** A rune is copied at its tier if the copier owns that tier, otherwise at their highest owned one; a rune they can't use (an unavailable limited-copy rune) leaves its socket empty. Common is always owned, so a socket only ever empties for availability. A spell the copier hasn't learned can't be copied at all, and says so.
  - Placement data isn't copied — a spot belongs to the original — so Bearing and Anchor aim afresh.
- **Bindings:** copy is middle mouse on keyboard and mouse. Edit's key and both controller bindings are open; Interact is taken by Manual activation. Both belong on the Ritual look prompt and in the hint bar.

The field HUD (`AbilityHud`, holding `TokenBar` and `AbilityBar` as independent siblings rather than one combined class) is two permanent rows — 3 token slots colored by spell element and labeled, above 4 ability slots showing each ability's name and a cooldown-fraction fill. Each slot's badge names its binding on the current device, and each row has a modifier chip at its left ([controls_and_ui.md](controls_and_ui.md)). On keyboard only one row shows badges at a time, since they share the `1`/`2`/`3` keys: abilities normally, tokens while pick mode is active — each row decides its own badge visibility off the same `TokenPickModeChanged` signal, with no shared state between them. On a controller the two rows' buttons never overlap, so both always show.

**Crosshair cues (`CrosshairCue`).** An ability can show state around the crosshair, read from `Ability` each frame so any Sentinel can opt in. A circle 5× the crosshair's size stays empty; everything sits outside it.

- **Shots left** while an ability holds the attack binding: Health Link's two arcs flank the crosshair and go black left then right as spikes are fired; Overdrive's shots are a row of bars below it in the dominant element's color, going black from the right.
- **Time bars** below that, one row per ability that has one, in fixed order so a bar's position says whose it is: Weak-Point Visor's duration, Health Link's arming window, Overdrive's expiry. They sit just clear of Attunement's element ring, which can be open while one runs.

**Navigation is gamepad-first**, not mouse-first-then-retrofit.

## Radial construction menu

**The wheel itself is built:** `RadialWheel` (`ui/radial/`) is a reusable Control that draws a ring of wedges, turns a direction into a highlighted slot and commits it, knowing nothing about what its items are. `BuildMenu` hosts all the wheels and `RitualInscription`; the rest of this section is the design they're being built toward.

- **Stick input.** The left stick reaches it through the `radial_left`/`radial_right`/`radial_up`/`radial_down` actions — its own actions rather than `move_*`, which includes WASD and would walk a keyboard player's character — so rebinding it to the right stick later is only an Input Map change.
- **Mouse input.** The mouse can't be bound to an action, so it has its own path into the same pointing code. A panel pushed with `UiPanelStack.Push(..., capturesMouse: true)` keeps the mouse captured, and `PlayerCharacter.HandleLook` ignores mouse motion while any panel is open, so pointing at a wedge never turns the camera.
- **Commit is on press:** an empty or disabled wedge can be highlighted but not committed. Because a press can start placement, `RitualPlacer` won't let a `confirm_placement` press that was already held when placement began confirm it — it polls input rather than receiving events, so marking the click handled wouldn't stop it.
- **Backing out belongs to the host**, which knows whether that means the previous ring or closing.
- **Host switches.** `DrawWedges` off leaves only the pointing (the host draws its own controls), and `HandlesCommit` off leaves presses to the host (for telling a tap from a hold). `ReplaceItems` swaps a wheel's items without resetting where it's pointing, for an item that changes mid-gesture.
- **Given more than 8 items**, a `RadialWheel` becomes a spiral (below) on its own, reporting item indexes rather than wedges, so hosts don't change.

**Two kinds of wheel.** _Wedge wheels_ are plain `RadialWheel`s — the element, spell, favorites and rune rings and the tier wheel — and commit on press. _Button wheels_ derive from `ButtonWheel`, which wraps a `RadialWheel` with wedges and commit both off and adds the layer on top: buttons placed around a center and lit when pointed at, tap versus hold (with a fill on rectangular buttons and a progress ring just outside round ones), a pointer dot, an optional backdrop circle with outlined wedges showing each slot's share of the pointing, center text, fading all but one slot, and relabeling a slot temporarily. A subclass supplies only its layout — slot count, each slot's position, size, shape and pointing weight — so the geometry lives with it; an empty slot still takes its weight, so a weight of 0 leaves a direction unreachable. `RitualInscription` and `TokenWheel` are the two so far.

Replaces the construction menu's tile grids — spell grid, Favorites tab, rune picker and tier popup — with radial wheels. The grids get clunky as the spell, rune and favorite counts scale, and a wheel makes picking a build a physical motion: the goal is a **marking menu**, where an experienced player stops reading the wheel and just performs the gesture ("up, then right" is Chain Lightning), which is also what gives building a tower the feel of casting a spell. The Token deployment flow (pick mode mid-wave) is untouched.

**Positions never move unless the player moves them.** Muscle memory only forms if an item is always in the same direction, so almost every rule below follows from this one:

- A spell's slot is fixed, not packed alphabetically — learning a new spell never shifts the others.
- **Unlearned spells show as locked slots** in their fixed position. Undecided against the alternative of the pie gaining slices as spells are learned; the fixed 8-position geometry below keeps positions stable either way.
- The element ring is built with its **Physical** and **multi-element** wedges from the start, dimmed until a spell exists for them, so the first one arriving doesn't rotate every element's angle.

**Structure.**

- **Every wheel aims for 8 wedges.** Eight puts every wedge on a cardinal or a diagonal — the easiest directions to hit reliably on a stick — and means a direction is the same place on every wheel.
- **Spell wheel.** Ring 1 is 8 wedges: **Favorites** at the top (the pinned favorites wheel, below — the most-used thing gets the most prominent direction), then clockwise Fire, Lightning, Cold, Water, Wind, Physical, and a **multi-element** wedge (gold until a wedge can draw a rainbow). Ring 2 is that element's spells in their slots. Ice Wall is Cold. The Favorites wedge is meant to carry a star icon once wedges can draw icons; it's a text label until then, since font coverage of symbols varies.
  - **A spell's wedge is worked out from its tags**, not authored: one element tag puts it under that element, none puts it under Physical, and more than one (Cataclysm) puts it under multi-element. Each spell has exactly one home, which keeps custom ordering simple. Listing a multi-element spell at the end of each of its elements instead is the alternative if players expect to find it there.
  - **Default order is an authored number on each `Spell`**, currently the order spells were added in development, to be revisited once there's a real unlock order. The Acquired sort needs nothing new: `KnownSpells` is appended to as spells are learned, so it's already in acquisition order.
- **Favorites are nested under their spell**, not in a separate wheel: element → spell → [New build | Favorite A | Favorite B …]. A favorite is a spell plus runes, so it lives with its spell; the ring under any one spell stays small; and "my Fireball chain build" becomes a three-flick incantation starting with the same motion as plain Fireball. A separate Favorites wheel filtered by element was considered and dropped — it bunches every Fire favorite into one ring, which overflows fast.
  - **New build** is the top slot; favorites follow in saved order, so a new one never moves the others. Picking a favorite goes straight to placement; one with a currently unavailable rune is dimmed. Backing out of the inscription lands on New build.
  - **A spell with no favorites skips the ring** and goes straight to a new build, for now. The cost is that the gesture for a new build of that spell gains a flick once its first favorite is saved; always showing the ring, with New build alone at the top, is the alternative if that proves jarring.
- **Pinned favorites wheel.** One static 8-slot wheel of favorites, for truly fast access to the towers a player uses most. It never spirals, and every favorite on it is still reachable under its own spell too — pinning only adds a shortcut.
  - **It fills itself automatically:** the player's first 8 favorites land in its slots as they're created, and on load any favorites not yet pinned backfill free slots in saved order — which is also how favorites saved before the wheel existed get onto it. The element ring's Favorites wedge is dimmed until something is pinned. From there the player customizes it — pinning, unpinning and choosing which slot each pin sits in, since the direction is the whole point. There's no sorting on this wheel, and an empty slot stays empty rather than collapsing. Deleting a favorite clears its pin.
  - **Auto-fill stops for good once the player takes charge of the wheel** — one flag per profile. Taking charge means deciding what a slot holds: pinning a favorite into a slot (empty, or replacing what's there), or unpinning one. Once a player has said "I want to be in charge of where things go," the wheel is theirs and new favorites no longer land on it on their own. Swapping filled slots doesn't count, so rearranging auto-filled favorites keeps auto-fill running; neither does deleting a favorite, which empties its slot for the next new favorite to fill. Tracking this as a single flag was chosen over marking individual slots as claimed, since it's simpler to explain to players.
  - **Reached through the element ring's Favorites wedge**, so it needs no binding of its own and a pinned favorite is two flicks away, like any spell. The spell wheel opens by default; a setting makes the pinned wheel open first instead, for one-flick access, with backing out of it landing on the element ring. The default is a fixed rule or the setting, never dependent on whether anything is pinned.
  - Selecting a pin behaves like any favorite: straight to placement, or stamping the token slot while authoring a token. Affordability shows the same way as everywhere else.
  - **Saved as a fixed 8-entry list of favorite names**, not the favorites themselves. A favorite is a tagged class written out in full wherever it appears, so a pin holding one would save a second copy that loads as a separate object and drifts from the original. Names are already unique (collisions are auto-suffixed). It's `PlayerSaveData.PinnedFavorites`, padded to 8 on load like Tokens; an empty slot is an empty string rather than a missing entry, so the load-time gap removal never collapses it. A pin naming no current favorite shows as empty. `PinnedWheelCustomized` is the taken-charge flag.
- **Ritual Inscription** (`RitualInscription`) — where runes are inscribed onto a spell, the step that makes it a complete ritual. Picking a spell (or New build) opens it: the spell at the center, the 4 sockets on the diagonals — numbered from top-left, clockwise, which is the order the first empty socket is offered and a favorite is named in — and the build's actions on the cardinals.
  - **It's a `ButtonWheel`**, so it's picked by direction exactly like a wedge wheel, over the same 8 directions, but **it doesn't look like one**: sockets are 182px circles and actions are buttons in an ordinary layout, lit up with a white border as they're pointed at.
  - **Unlike the other wheels it sits at screen center**, on a dark ~1070px circle behind its controls rather than a full-screen dim; the circle is what to replace when there's art for it.
  - **Sizes** are in the project's 2560×1440 base resolution, which Godot scales to the real one (`canvas_items` stretch, `expand` aspect), so the menu's layout has that whole space to work with at any resolution.
  - **Sockets are easier to point at than actions.** A wheel item can carry a weight — its share of the angle relative to its neighbors — and sockets weigh twice an action, so each socket answers to 60° of pointing and each action 30°. Every item stays centered on its compass direction; weight only moves the boundaries between neighbors. Diagonals are the hardest directions to land on a stick, and sockets are what gets picked most.
  - **A pointer dot** shows where the stick or mouse points, semi-transparent and drawn behind the center text, since the inscription hides its wheel's wedges and with them the wheel's own dot.
  - **Actions on the cardinals rather than face buttons**, so a keyboard-and-mouse player can do everything by pointing and clicking, and no confirmation popup is needed (a popup would want a visible cursor, which a captured mouse doesn't have). Placed where they feel natural on a controller, echoing the PlayStation face buttons' positions: **bottom Place** (confirm the build and go to placement), **right Clear All**, **top Save Favorite**, **left Save as Token**. They're directions only — Cross still presses whatever is highlighted, so the face buttons can't double as shortcuts without breaking that.
  - **It tells a tap from a hold**, reading presses itself rather than leaving them to its wheel. Holds are specific to the inscription; every other wheel stays commit-on-press, since those are meant to be as quick as possible. A slot with no hold time fires on press; one with a hold time fires its tap on release, or its hold once pressed that long, and pointing away mid-hold cancels.
    - **A filled socket:** tap swaps its rune (straight to the rune wheel), hold past the same 0.3s threshold ability slots use opens the tier wheel. An empty socket goes to the rune wheel on press.
    - **Clear All is hold-to-confirm for 1s**, like repair and destroy, with the button filling as it's held; a tap just says to hold. A socket's hold shows as a ring filling around the socket instead, since a filling rectangle inside a circle reads as a glitch.
  - **Save Favorite** saves instantly under a default name and says which (see Favorites above).
  - **Save as Token** (left) opens the token wheel to pick a slot, from an ordinary build. While authoring a token (below) the inscription changes instead: the bottom action becomes **Save Token**, and the left becomes **Clear Token**, held 1s to empty the token and enabled only when it holds one.
  - **Flow:** it opens with the first empty socket highlighted, or Place once every socket is full. After a rune is socketed it reopens with the next empty socket highlighted, so filling a build is one continuous chain. Backing out returns to the spell ring with that spell highlighted.
  - **The center** shows the spell's name and total cost against current mana, plus a line about the highlighted socket or action.
- **Rune wheel.** Ring 1 is a rune bucket, ring 2 the compatible runes in it, each at its highest owned tier, colored by rarity. Swapping a socket's rune opens it highlighting that rune if it's in the bucket picked. The buckets are temporary (below).
  - **One bucket has a sub-bucket:** Targeting's ring opens with a **Status** wedge first, then the targeting runes outside it; Status opens a ring of the per-element status targeting runes. This is the only nesting, kept to one level (`RuneTags.SubBucketsOf`). A rune carrying a sub-bucket's tag shows only inside it, and the parent ring highlights the sub-bucket when the socket's rune is in there.
  - **It opens around the socket being filled, drawn over the inscription**: the socket itself is the ring's hole (plus a small gap so its highlight border shows), with wedges about as thick as the spell wheel's. The bucket ring, rune ring and tier wheel all open there, so inscribing reads as happening _at that spot_. The rest of the inscription fades while it's open, the socket's label previews the rune pointed at, and the inscription's center cost shows the total with it.
  - **The rune's name, cost change and description sit in a wide panel on the ring's side of the inscription** — above it for the top two sockets, below it for the bottom two — with name and cost on one row and the description under them, since the ring overlaps the inscription's center text. A description that runs long grows the panel away from the ring, never into it.
- **Tier wheel.** Holding a filled socket opens a small 8-slot wheel: **Swap** at the top (opens the rune wheel for this socket — likely the most common reason to open a filled socket), then clockwise from the top-right an arc of **Common, Uncommon, Rare, Epic, Legendary**, rarity rising clockwise, and **Clear** (unsocket) on the left. Tiers not owned are dimmed, never hidden, so positions never change. It opens with the socket's current tier highlighted. A bumper shortcut for stepping tiers inside the rune wheel was considered; the keyboard-and-mouse equivalent would collide with the scroll wheel (used for spiral laps, below), so it's left out unless picking a lower tier proves slow in play.
- **Backing out** steps one screen at a time: tier wheel or bucket ring → inscription, sub-bucket ring → its bucket's ring, rune ring → bucket ring, inscription → spell ring. `Q` closes the whole menu from anywhere.
- **Later, maybe: flick-and-release.** For the quick wheels, moving the stick and releasing it could commit with no button press at all. Button-press commit comes first to see how it feels.

- **Token wheel** (`TokenWheel`, a `ButtonWheel`). Three round buttons at **left, top and right** — tokens 1, 2 and 3, matching the field's d-pad left/up/right and keys 1/2/3. It's never meant to hold 8, so it breaks the 8-wedge rule on purpose. Its four directions are weighted 2 / 1 / 0 / 1 (up, right, down, left), splitting the pointing into a top, right and left third, with down unreachable; a fourth token would give down a real weight. Each token's share of the pointing is drawn as an outlined wedge behind its button, always visible, and the pointed-at wedge lights up rather than the button. The field HUD's token row stays visible under the menu, so the wheel doesn't repeat it. The center shows the pointed-at token's runes and deploy cost.
  - **Reaching it:** R1 inside the menu opens it to pick a token to author; 1/2/3 (or R1 with the d-pad) skip it and go straight to that token. The inscription's Save as Token opens it to choose where the current build goes.
  - **Choosing where to save a build:** tapping an empty slot saves there; a filled slot needs a 1s hold to replace, and a tap just says so. Then it's back to the inscription, which can still place the build.
  - **Authoring a token** puts the menu in token mode.
    - **Marking:** a gold ring around whichever wheel is up — outside the wedges of a wedge wheel, along the inscription's backdrop circle — says something is different, and a gold "Token N" tag says what. The tag sits at a fixed spot just left of the menu, the one place nothing else ever appears (the rune info panel is always above or below the inscription, and the rune rings stay inside its circle), so it never collides with another label.
    - **Starting point:** a filled token opens the inscription pre-filled with its build; an empty one starts at the element ring. Picking a favorite stamps the token with its build and name instead of placing it.
    - **Limited-copy runes**, and favorites holding one, are dimmed, since a token may never hold a limited rune.
    - **Saving or clearing** a token picked on the token wheel returns there with that slot highlighted; one reached straight from 1/2/3 (or R1 with the d-pad) closes the menu.
    - **Backing out:** a token reached straight from 1/2/3 closes the menu when backed out of the screen it opened on — the element ring for an empty token, the inscription for a filled one; otherwise backing out of the element ring in token mode returns to the token wheel; backing out of the token wheel lands on the ordinary element ring.
  - **A token is named after the favorite holding exactly its build**, if one exists, and otherwise shows its spell's name. Saving an edited favorite-stamped token unchanged keeps the name; editing it into a different build drops it rather than mislabeling it; a hand-built token matching a favorite picks the name up.
  - **Token authoring reuses the menu's in-progress build** — the selected spell and sockets — as the grid menu did, so editing a token and backing out leaves its build in the inscription.

**Save data.** The arranged orders (`SpellOrder`, `RuneOrder`; favorites keep theirs in `Favorites` itself), the pinned wheel and its taken-charge flag are `[Export]` fields on the profile, and sort mode and the which-wheel-opens-first setting will be too. Adding fields needs no schema version or migration — old saves load them at their defaults ([architecture.md](architecture.md), Save format) — so none of this waits on save work.

**The center of the wheel** replaces the description panel: the hovered item's art in the background with its name, description and cost overlaid. Anything that won't fit goes on the outer edge. Costs are the **resolved** cost against current mana — what the player actually needs is whether they can afford it — with the rune wheel showing the cost change a rune would add. Wedges the player can't currently afford are dimmed or carry a red cost.

**Input.**

- **The stick picks the wedge, Cross commits.** The highlight stays on the last wedge when the stick returns to center, so the player can let go and then press. **Circle backs out** one level, and out of the menu from the top.
- **The mouse acts as a virtual stick:** mouse movement accumulates into a direction clamped to the wheel's radius, so it's the same gesture as the controller. That needs raw mouse movement, so **the mouse stays captured while a wheel is open** — a deliberate exception to the rule that the mouse is released while a panel is open ([controls_and_ui.md](controls_and_ui.md)).
- **On a mouse, returning to center highlights nothing**, so a click there does nothing. Nothing lets go of a mouse the way a stick springs back, so there's no reason to keep the highlight. A highlight the wheel opens with (the inscription's next empty socket) stays until the player points somewhere.
- **Flick-and-return** (a flick past a threshold commits as the stick springs back, no button press) is deferred until the wheel exists to try it on.
- Bindings in general aren't settled.

**Rings with more than 8 items become a spiral.** Past about 8 wedges, picking the right one with a stick gets unreliable, but a player with dozens of favorites under one spell still needs to reach them all without paging through menus.

- **There are always 8 fixed wedge positions**, and item _n_ always appears at wedge _n_ mod 8 — item 9 where item 1 was, item 10 where item 2 was. The direction a player learns for an item stays true on every lap.
- **The first lap is absolute**, so a ring of 8 or fewer items is pure flick-to-slot and never spirals.
- **Rotating continuously past the end of a lap scrolls to the next one.** The wedges don't move; their contents swap, fading from the outgoing item to the incoming one, so the list progresses without the wheel itself rotating.
- **The seam moves with the stick**: the break between laps always sits directly opposite it, the wedges ahead showing upcoming items and the ones behind showing what's been passed, fading by distance along the spiral. Chosen over a fixed seam between wedges 8 and 1, where wedge 1 would abruptly stop being item 1. A lap counter at the bottom of the center ("2 / 3") and a glowing line in the gap at the seam sell the spiral; the line marks any break between neighboring wedges, so it also shows where the list ends.
- **It stops at the last item** rather than wrapping. Rotating backward past item 1 also stops.
- **A flick lands on whatever the wedge is currently showing**, never jumping laps.
- **Returning the stick to center keeps the current lap** — the mouse too, even though it drops the highlight. Stepping back a lap means rotating backward — an accepted inconvenience, since spirals only exist for players with a lot of content.
- On a mouse, the virtual stick handles laps the same way, and the scroll wheel jumps a whole lap.

**Sort modes and custom order.** A sort mode is a saved preference, not a toggle — a player picks one and keeps it, which makes it just a different fixed layout and keeps muscle memory intact. The element ring never sorts; modes apply inside it (spells within an element, favorites within a spell, runes within a group).

- **Default** (authored order), **Alphabetical**, **Acquired** (order obtained), and **Custom**. Acquired is fully stable — new things always land at the end — while Alphabetical inserts mid-list and shifts what follows. Mana cost was considered and dropped: the base cost isn't what a player is weighing, and the resolved cost changes with gear.
- **Most Used (idea, not prioritized):** spells and runes ordered by how often this profile has placed them, most frequent first. Needs per-profile usage counts. It's the one mode that isn't a fixed layout — an item climbs past another as counts change — so when it reorders (live, or only between matches) is open.
- **Custom order is set on the Organize panel** (below), not by rearranging wedges inside the wheel. Until sort modes exist, the arranged order simply _is_ the order; sort modes will make it the Custom mode.
- **Sort mode will be changed on the Organize panel.** Not on a bare R3 press inside the wheel, since an accidental click mid-gesture would rearrange everything.

**Organize panel (built).** `OrganizePanel` (`ui/organize/`): one panel with a tab per thing arranged — **Spell Wheels, Favorites, Runes** — opened by the character sheet's Organize row, whose three buttons each land on their tab. Works in the hub and in a level. An ordinary panel rather than a wheel, so the mouse is free and Godot's focus navigation drives it on a controller.

- **Reordering** (`ReorderList`) works the same on every device: press an entry to pick it up, move it with up/down or the mouse wheel, press again to put it down — or click another entry to drop it there. **Back** undoes one step at a time (a held entry goes back where it was, a slot being chosen is dropped) before it closes the panel.
- **Spell Wheels:** an element, then its spells in ring order. Saved as `SpellOrder`; a spell never arranged sits after the arranged ones in authored order, so learning a spell adds it at the end without moving the others.
- **Favorites:** the spells that have favorites, then that spell's favorites in ring order, each with **Rename, Pin/Unpin and Delete**; beside them the pinned wheel's 8 slots, laid out in the directions they take on the wheel.
  - **Rename** turns the entry into a text field (Enter saves, Back or clicking away cancels). An empty name, or one another favorite has, is refused. The pinned wheel and any token named after the favorite follow it to the new name.
  - **Delete** takes two presses — the first arms the button for 3s. It empties the favorite's pin slot, and a token named after it falls back to its spell's name.
  - **Pin, then a slot,** puts the favorite there, replacing whatever held it; one already pinned elsewhere moves. **A pinned slot, then another,** swaps the two.
- **Runes:** one global order that every bucket shows its runes in — a rune is arranged once even when it sits in several buckets, and as a rune, so every tier takes the same place. Each row lists the buckets the rune shows in. Saved as `RuneOrder`; runes never arranged follow by name.

**Open.**

- **Rune buckets — temporary, subject to change.** The rune wheel's first ring is 8 fixed buckets, clockwise from the top: **Trajectory, Area, Targeting, Cooldown, Reaction, Trigger, Tradeoff, Utility.** They're broad on purpose. An Element bucket was dropped because nearly every elemental rune will be a reaction rune anyway. Targeting took the slot of a Duration bucket that never held a rune; `Tag.Duration` remains a spell tag.
  - **A rune's buckets come from its own tags.** Runes gain `Rune.Tags` — what kind of rune it is — separate from `RequiredTags`/`RequiredAnyTags`, which stay the compatibility check against a spell. This is PoE's support-gem tagging: a tag can exist mostly for sorting and still give a later item or unique something to hook ("Tradeoff runes have 50% less downside").
  - **One shared `Tag` enum.** The rune-only values (Trajectory, Cooldown, Reaction, Tradeoff, Utility, Targeting, and the TargetStatus sub-bucket; Trigger already existed) join the same enum spells use, so a cross-cutting effect can name "Projectile" once for spells and runes alike. The Area bucket reuses `Tag.AoE` rather than adding a near-duplicate. The compiler can't stop a spell carrying a rune-only tag or a rune requiring one — which would make that rune incompatible with every spell and silently absent — so a startup check throws on both.
  - **A rune can carry several bucket tags** and then shows in each of those wedges. It must carry at least one, or it would appear nowhere; the same startup check enforces that. Organizing a much larger roster better is a problem for when it exists.
  - **Compatibility still filters inside a bucket**: only runes that fit the chosen spell appear. Tags decide that, unless a rune overrides `Rune.IsCompatibleWith(Spell)` for a rule tags can't express: **Haste fits only spells without a cooldown of their own and Tempo only spells with one**, so no spell ever offers a socket that does next to nothing (Haste barely shortens an 8s-cooldown Ice Storm's cycle). A cooldown a rune adds, like Manual activation's, doesn't count for either. **The rune wheel always opens with its spell already chosen**, so muscle memory forms per spell. Within a bucket runes follow the player's rune order (the Organize panel's Runes tab).
  - **Today's runes:** Trajectory — Salvo, Pierce, Nova, Return, Velocity, Chain, Fork. Area — Expand. Cooldown — Haste, Tempo. Reaction — Ignition, Overload, Frostburn, Melt, Shatter, Freeze, Electrocute. Utility — Chill, Shroud, Extend (the Ritual's reach, which is why it isn't under Area — that means AoE radius). Trigger — Manual Activation, Resonance, Far Resonance, Fast Resonance ([runes.md](runes.md)). Targeting — Proximity, Horizon, Giantslayer, Cull, Swarm; its Status sub-bucket — Embersight, Frostsight, Tidesight, Stormsight, Omen ([runes.md](runes.md)). Tradeoff is empty for now.
  - Groupings considered before these: by the chosen spell's own tags plus an any-spell group (compatibility-first, but runes that fit every spell piled into one large group), and by what a rune does with an extra element ring for elemental groups.
- **Greying.** Within a bucket, a rune that conflicts with one already socketed (same type, or a second reaction rune) or is unavailable dims **in place** rather than sorting to the back, since moving runes around as sockets fill works against muscle memory. **A bucket dims when nothing in it can be picked** for the socket being filled — no compatible rune, or every one conflicting or unavailable, like a Cooldown bucket whose only fitting rune is already in another socket. Whether unlearned runes show dimmed or hidden is open.
